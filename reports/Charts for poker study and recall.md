# Draw the answer, not the range

The owner's instinct is right, and the research says why: a chart teaches and is remembered when the answer to the question is a single thing you can see (a line, a gap, a named shape) on a frame that never changes, in about three or four parts. The 13x13 grid and the equity curve were not built that way. The grid is a lookup index sorted by card rank, and the curve answers one kind of question well. Both the literature and the tool's own experiments on 8,435 solved decisions point to the same replacement for **defending against a bet and for river betting**: a range sorted by strength with one or two cut lines, which accounts for 69 to 96% of what hands do, against the grid's 73 to 87% with about thirty cells. They part company on **flop and turn betting**, where the simple strip the literature would favour accounts for only 38 to 45% and would mislead; there a second axis (how much the hand changes on later cards) is needed, at a cost in novelty and memory load. The recommendation is a small family on one fixed frame: the line for defending, three bands for river betting, a two-by-three settled/unsettled map for earlier betting, five named shapes as the vocabulary for storing a spot, and one count (who has more hands above about 70% equity) as the at-the-table landmark for betting direction. The charts should be taught by prediction, drawing from memory and quick classification drills, not by display. Three limits apply throughout. The researchers read abstracts and search summaries, not full papers, because full-text fetches were blocked. No peer-reviewed study of any poker strategy visual exists. The tool's experiments tested no people: they show the answer is *in* the picture in few pieces, not that a player can learn, hold or use it. No chart, in either body of evidence, implies bet size.

## A chart helps when the answer is something you see, in three or four parts

### Two charts can hold the same facts and demand very different work

The founding result in this field is that two displays holding identical information can differ greatly in the effort needed to use them. A diagram helps when it groups related information by location and turns an inference into a perception, and only if it "is constructed to take advantage of these features" and the reader has been taught what to look for ([Larkin & Simon 1987](https://mechanism.ucsd.edu/bill/teaching/F12/cs200/Readings/larkin.whyadiagramissometimesworth.1987.pdf)). When the rule a task needs is built into the display, the task gets easier ([Zhang & Norman 1994](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~scoulson/203/zhang.pdf)). Diagrams redesigned on this principle have beaten conventional teaching in classroom experiments: electricity taught with rectangles whose width, height and area are the quantities gave larger gains on transfer problems than equations ([Cheng 2002](https://users.sussex.ac.uk/~peterch/papers/Cheng-CogSci26%286%29.pdf)), and in probability a square cut into areas beat a tree diagram in two experiments with 148 and 143 students ([Böcherer-Linder & Eichler 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.02026/text)). A follow-up with **688 undergraduates** found what actually helped: not area as such, but **countable objects** and making visible which group sits inside which ([Böcherer-Linder & Eichler 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00267/pdf)). The caveats: these are students in lessons, Cheng's work comes from one laboratory, and effect sizes could not be read.

### The eye reads position well and shade badly, and compares only what is lined up

People judge **position on a shared scale most precisely and colour shade least**, an ordering from 1984 that largely held up in a later online replication ([Cleveland & McGill summary](https://info3312.infosci.cornell.edu/slides/04-graphical-perception.html); [Heer & Bostock 2010](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/crowdsourcing-graphical-perception)). The ranking is an average that hides large differences between individuals ([Risks of Ranking](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.10533)). A major review adds that viewers pick up an overall impression (roughly where the mass is) in a fraction of a second, but **comparing one set of values against another is slow** unless the design lines the two up; it also recommends labels on the graphic, not in a legend, and familiar conventions ([Franconeri et al. 2021](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/visual-data-communication.html)). Comparison is faster and more accurate when two pictures are placed so matching parts sit directly opposite each other: two left-to-right pictures stacked, or two top-to-bottom pictures side by side ([Matlen, Gentner & Franconeri 2020](https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/gentner/papers/MatlenEtAl_2020-SpatialAlign.pdf)). Format also steers what people conclude. Bars prompt "this group against that group"; a connected line prompts "a trend" ([Zacks & Tversky 1999](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/bf03201236.pdf)).

For colour-coded grids the direct evidence is thin, and what there is says colour alone is weak for values: printing digits on the cells raised accuracy by **10 to 60 percentage points** ([Han & Nacenta 2020](https://graphicsinterface.org/proceedings/gi2020/gi2020-23/)). Grouping same-coloured items together, compared with scattering them, eases spotting trends and odd ones out ([Haroz & Whitney 2012](https://whitneylab.berkeley.edu/PDFs/Haroz_Whitney_2012_InfoVis.pdf)). The widely taught claim that sorting a matrix by a meaningful variable reveals patterns is a design principle with no controlled human test that the researchers could find.

For showing a spread of values, **countable marks beat smooth curves**. Icon arrays improved accuracy for both low- and high-numeracy readers ([Galesic et al. 2009](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_2099767_5/component/file_2562291/MG_Using_2009.pdf)). A distribution drawn as a fixed number of dots, each an equal slice of probability, gave steadier probability estimates than a density curve ([Kay et al. 2016](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/when-ish-is-my-bus)), and in a paid decision task a 50-dot version earned **97% of the best possible payoff** ([Fernandes et al. 2018](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/uncertainty-bus)). Cumulative curves, the family the poker equity curve belongs to, are a mixed case: they did nearly as well as dots for threshold decisions in that study, yet in an older one they "severely mislead some subjects in estimating the mean" ([Ibrekk & Morgan 1987](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:riskan:v:7:y:1987:i:4:p:519-529)). A bar showing an average makes values inside the bar look likelier than equally distant values outside it ([Newman & Scholl 2012](https://www.sci.utah.edu/~kpotter/Library/Papers/newman:2012:WTBB/index.html)). All of this evidence is from bus times and medical risk, not games.

### A pictured chart has a budget of about three or four parts

People hold about **three or four visual chunks** at once ([Cowan 2001](https://memory.psych.missouri.edu/assets/doc/articles/2001/cowan-bbs-2001.pdf)), and a mental image is weaker than that: imagery is "severely limited", with quality falling as items are added ([Keogh & Pearson 2017](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/cognitive-bias/illusion-of-depth/2017-keogh.pdf)). The best direct evidence that people can compute on an imagined picture is the mental abacus, and it sets the same budget: users store the abacus as columns, **cannot hold more than about three**, and adding beads inside a column costs nothing ([Frank & Barner 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21767040/)). Detail inside a part is cheap; extra parts are expensive. Capacity rises with structure, not effort: grouping by closeness improved memory accuracy by about 12% in one study ([review citing Woodman et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4068940)), and learned regularities let more be stored ([Brady et al. 2009](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/a9b32c42-474e-4d98-83cb-9213f6bccce7)).

Two further results matter for "visualise it at the table". First, a remembered image is hard to re-read for something it was not learned for: people could not flip the duck/rabbit figure in imagination, though they could once they drew it, and novel readings worked mainly for images built from simple parts ([Peterson et al. 1992](https://ocf.berkeley.edu/~jfkihlstrom/PDFs/1990s/1992/Peterson_etal1992.pdf); [Finke, Pinker & Farah 1989](https://stevenpinker.com/publications/reinterpreting-visual-patterns-mental-imagery)). Second, memory for a graph drifts toward the tidy version: near-symmetric curves are recalled as symmetric and lines as closer to 45 degrees ([Tversky & Schiano 1989](https://opact.sdu.edu.tr/vufind/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-a391t-a895675e27ba08337c115609670181625704ad80223f08e5c98b6fe68567ef13)). What people recall from a chart is mostly its title and text ([Borkin et al. 2016](https://vcg.seas.harvard.edu/publications/20150101-beyond-memorability-visualization-recognition-and-recall)). Layout survives better than colour: people with no pictorial imagery, roughly 1 to 4% of the population depending on the cut-off ([Dance et al. 2022](https://sro.sussex.ac.uk/id/eprint/103365/1/SIMNER_Consciousness_and_Cognition_NOV_2021.pdf)), drew scenes from memory with fewer objects and less colour but normal spatial accuracy ([Bainbridge et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7856239)).

### Experts remember a fixed frame plus a small vocabulary of patterns

Chess masters reconstruct real positions far better than novices but recall only three or four pieces of a random one, so their edge is learned patterns, not better memory ([Chase & Simon 1973](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/chess/1973-simon.pdf)). Those patterns are tied to place: recall suffers when the board is mirrored ([Gobet & Simon 1996](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03200937)). Common position types are held as "templates", a fixed core with slots for what varies ([Gobet 1998 review](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/815/1/FullText.pdf)). Memory champions use the same mechanism on purpose, hanging content on a fixed set of well-learned places; pooled across 13 trials the method has a medium effect, g = 0.65 ([Twomey & Kroneisen 2021](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/cmedm,33535926)). (As a rule of thumb, an effect of 0.5 puts the typical trained learner ahead of about seven in ten untrained ones.) People also abstract a prototype readily: after sorting distorted dot patterns they classified the never-seen original about as well as the ones they had studied ([Posner & Keele 1968 summary](https://support.pstnet.com/hc/en-us/articles/360050436814-STEP-On-the-Genesis-of-Abstract-Ideas-35277)).

Practice in neighbouring games offers one model worth copying and one warning. Backgammon players turn computer analysis into one adjusted count and memorised cut-offs: "double if your count exceeds your opponent's by no more than 4", and so on ([Keith count](https://bkgm.com/articles/Keith/KangarooCount/)). Short yes/no decision trees of about three questions matched a standard statistical model across 30 data sets ([Martignon et al., as reported in Jenny et al. 2013](https://dwulff.github.io/_Goodchoices/Literature/JennyEtAl2013SimpleRulesForDetectingDepression.pdf)) and beat doctors' judgement in a well-known heart-care case ([Marewski & Gigerenzer](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3341653)), though no study found measures how well people remember such trees. The warning is the candlestick chart: a named vocabulary of shapes that spread widely although the patterns did not predict returns ([Marshall, Young & Rose 2007](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ris/jofitr/0834.html)). Named shapes catch on whether or not they are true, so each one has to be checked against the solver. Data glyphs that pack many variables into a face or star have tested poorly; plain bars and lines do better ([Lee et al. 2003](https://crpit.scem.westernsydney.edu.au/abstracts/CRPITV24Lee.html); [Tableau Research](https://www.tableau.com/es-es/research/publications/ross-chernoff-glyphs-or-how-do-we-kill-bad-ideas-visualization)).

### Looking at a clear chart is not learning it

A clear display makes learners feel they understand, a problem researchers call "deceptive clarity" ([Linn et al. 2010](https://www.sri.com/publication/education-learning-pubs/stem-and-computer-science-pubs/can-desirable-difficulties-overcome-deceptive-clarity-in-scientific-visualizations/)). Making the important feature stand out gave **no accuracy benefit until viewers had been taught the subject** ([Hegarty et al. 2010](https://typeset.io/pdf/thinking-about-the-weather-how-display-salience-and-2wgkcgr4gn.pdf)). And designers overrate their own charts: people told a backstory assumed naive viewers would see the same pattern they did, even when warned ([Xiong et al. 2020](https://visualcognition.psych.northwestern.edu/publications/XiongCurse2020.pdf)). The specific remedies and their evidence are in the teaching section below.

## The grid holds the answer in thirty pieces; the curve answers one question well

### The grid is an index that arrived with range software

The only dated account of the grid's origin found is a poker author saying that when he wrote his 2007 book "the now-ubiquitous 13×13 grid of hold'em starting hands did not exist" ([Tommy Angelo](https://www.tommyangelo.com/ushc)). Who first drew it is not established. It indexes the 169 starting hands by card rank. Nothing found suggests it was designed or tested for retention, which supports the owner's reading.

Judged against the research, it has two real strengths and four weaknesses; all of these judgements are inferences, since nobody has tested the grid. The strengths: it is a **fixed frame with landmarks** (the pairs diagonal, suited above, offsuit below) that every player already knows, which is exactly what expert memory hangs patterns on, and it is the best lookup by hand name. The weaknesses: it is sorted by rank, not by anything the strategy depends on after the flop, so like-coloured cells scatter (the harder condition in the grouping research). Its cells look equal but hold 6, 4 or 12 combinations, so the area of a colour misstates its share of the range, a flaw practitioners already flag ([vip-grinders](https://www.vip-grinders.com/poker-strategy/ranges/)). Mixed frequencies shown by shade use the least precise channel. And "who has more strong hands" or "how often does the range bet" needs a search and a weighted sum that the geometry does not do for you.

The tool's experiments add the decisive number, and it is fairer to the grid than the owner's complaint. The grid *does* contain the answer: its roughly thirty cells in play account for 67 to 87% of what hands do, and for flop and turn betting no one-dimensional view beat it. Its fault is the piece count. Thirty cells is about eight times the three-or-four-part budget, and a 169-cell coloured grid cannot be sketched from memory, which closes off the drawing route to retention. Coaches already work around this by teaching players to memorise border hands, not cells ([BetterPoker](https://betterpoker.vercel.app/learn/preflop-mastery/preflop-in-practice/memorizing-ranges)): they compress the grid to a boundary line by hand.

### The equity curve is better than "an information dump", but only for one job

The curve plots each hand's equity against its rank within the range, one line per player ([GTO Wizard](https://blog.gtowizard.com/interpreting-equity-distributions/)). The research predicts it should be good at threshold questions (what share of my range clears this bar) and poor at averages and overall shape, and that two overlaid curves must be compared by eye, which is the slow kind of comparison. The tool's experiments match this closely. For **facing a bet**, five points of your own curve were the best simple picture tested, leaving a simple rule 6.4 points off on continue frequency, against 9.7 from the situation alone and 8.3 from two-range pictures. For **betting**, the curve was worse than pictures of both ranges (14.1 points off against about 13.3). So the curve earns its place for defending, where the question is where your range sits against the price.

Its weaknesses for study are real. It shows equity, not action, so the answer is never on it. Drawn as a line it invites a "trend" reading when the strategic message is "this group against that group". Memory will straighten it toward the diagonal and erase the bumps that matter. Tools disagree on which end is strong ([PLO Mastermind](https://intercom.help/plo-mastermind/en/articles/12129411-4-card-plo-trainer-equity-graphs)), and the shape names attached to it (linear, merged, condensed) are used inconsistently between writers ([DeucesCracked](https://www.deucescracked.com/blog/polarized-vs-merged-ranges-poker-betting-guide-2026); [pokerskill](https://www.pokerskill.com/blog/polarized-linear-merged-ranges/)). It also marks hands above 90% equity as the "nut advantage" cue; in the tool's experiments marking the 90% top separately **added nothing** to predicting bet frequency once the 70% mark was known.

## Six candidates: where the tool's numbers and the literature agree, and where they do not

The tool's evidence is in `research/charts/README.md`: four experiments on 8,435 solved decisions from 132 flops and their later streets. "Accounts for X%" means that of the differences between hands in what they do, the pieces of the picture explain X%. Two cautions on reading it. The cut lines were placed at the best positions for each decision, so the result shows the answer has that shape, not that a viewer would know where the cuts go. And no people were tested.

| Design | Literature says | Tool's experiments say | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Two aligned strips | Strong fit: position on a shared scale, direct alignment | Best simple picture for betting direction; worse than own curve for defending; silent on size | Build, as the frame for the others |
| B. The line | Ideal: one visible boundary is the answer; one part to hold | 69 to 88% with one cut, up to 96% with two on the river; beats the grid | Build first |
| C. Five named shapes | Good fit for prototypes and templates; must be validated | Five shapes cover 60% of how strips differ | Build as vocabulary, with honest limits |
| D. Settled/unsettled map | Costly: novel chart, six regions exceed the budget | Needed: 66% and 79%, where the strip gets 38% and 45% | Build, staged, as a 2-by-3 |
| E. Four-box card | Good fit: countable, nested, four parts | No better for a simple reader; fixed cuts hurt when defending | Fold into the count, not a main chart |
| F. Small copies per next card | No learning evidence found; fails the memory budget | Not tested | Study drill only, grouped to three or four |

### B, the line: the clearest agreement

This is where the two bodies of evidence agree most strongly. The literature asks for the answer to be one visible feature, held as one part, encoded at learning time because images are hard to re-read later. The experiments find that facing a bet, **one cut on a strength-sorted strip accounts for 69% (flop), 82% (turn) and 88% (river)** of what hands do, two cuts for 75%, 88% and 96%, and that the only shape is a line with everything above it continuing. The grid needs about thirty cells to reach 73%, 80% and 87%. A coach's nine hand types do worse than either (61 to 75%). Two refinements come from the experiments. On the flop the sort key should include the draw: adding it lifts the strip from 69% to 80% with one cut. And five positions of the line cover 67% of cases, so "where does the line sit" has a small set of answers to learn. One inference from the diagram research: the price of the bet can be drawn on the same scale, so that "a bigger bet moves the line up" is something the picture does, in the way Cheng's diagrams build the law into the geometry. Untested.

### A, two strips: agreement on betting direction, a small conflict on defending

The alignment and common-scale research supports two strips placed so equal strength sits opposite. The experiments support it for betting: two-range pictures beat one curve, and the **best single thing to read is who has more hands above about 70% equity**. They conflict with the literature's general preference for showing both players when defending: there, your own range against the price beat any two-range picture. So the opponent's strip earns its place for betting decisions and is clutter for calling decisions (an inference from the numbers).

The honest size of the benefit is modest. From the situation alone (street, bet faced, stacks, seat) a simple rule gets the direction of betting (mostly check, mixed, mostly bet) right 63% of the time; with any picture of the two ranges it reaches **71%**, and stays about **13 points off** on the frequency. A picture of ranges gives direction, not a number. The strips' larger value is at hand level: showing which hands do what.

### C, five shapes: validated more than candlesticks, less than the names suggest

The prototype, template and labelling research all favour storing a spot as a name plus a deviation, and about five is within the four-to-six the memory researcher judged discriminable (that range is a judgement, not a finding). The experiments supply what candlesticks never had: a check against the truth. Five shapes cover 60% of how 5,099 betting strips differ. Three cautions, the last two being inferences. First, 40% is not covered, so some spots fit no name. Second, the commonest shape (27%) is not "top only": the top bets about 70% and **everything else bets about a third**. Memory tidies shapes toward their label, so a name like "top only" will teach players that the rest checks. Call it something like "top heavy, the rest a third". Third, the shapes describe how often each strength step bets, not which hands within a step. On the flop and turn the experiments show strength does not decide that, so a shape is a range-level summary and must be paired with the map for hand-level answers.

### D, the map: the main conflict, resolved in favour of the data

Left to the literature alone, one would draw a strength strip for everything: it is the simplest picture and fits the budget. The experiments say that would mislead. For **flop and turn betting three strength bands account for only 38% and 45%**, and other sort keys change little. A second axis, how much the hand changes with cards to come, cut into six regions, lifts this to **66% and 79%**, about what the grid does with thirty cells. No particular measure of "change" is special, so the one easiest to explain can be chosen; outs alone is the weakest.

The costs the literature predicts are real. The map is a new chart type, and novices facing an unfamiliar chart must first build a frame for it and can flounder ([Lee et al. 2016](https://www.aminer.org/pub/5687e80c0cf2afdcd166006b/how-do-people-make-sense-of-unfamiliar-visualizations-a-grounded-model-of)). Six regions is over the three-or-four budget. The suggested way through is an inference from the abacus result: draw it as a regular lattice, three strength rows by two columns (settled, unsettled), so the player holds three rows with a left-right split inside each, not six separate things. Even then a third of flop betting is unexplained, and whether anyone can picture this at a table is unknown.

### E, the four-box card: the literature likes it more than the data does

Four countable blocks, nested in a whole, in blocks that prompt "this group against that group": on paper this is what the probability-diagram and memory research favour, and solver products already ship equity buckets. The experiments are cooler. For a flexible statistical reader the card was the best picture for betting (9.0 points off), but for a simple rule of the kind a person can hold it was no better than the strips (13.5 against 13.3), and when facing a bet its fixed cut lines made it worse than the curve (8.3 against 6.4), because the line that matters moves with the price and the boxes do not. The useful part of the card is one of its counts, the hands above about 70%, which the strips can carry as a tick mark.

### F, small copies for each next card: a study aid, not a memory aid

No experiment on small multiples was retrieved, and the tool did not test them. The alignment research supports the layout if every copy shares the frame and only the card differs. Animation research adds that motion helps most when the change itself is what must be learned (average benefit small, g about 0.23; [Berney & Bétrancourt 2016](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:92234)), which fits a strip re-sorting from flop to turn. But a dozen strips is far outside what can be pictured, and on a phone they will be tiny. The inference is to group next cards into three or four kinds, stack those few strips aligned, and use them as a "what moved?" drill.

## What to build: one frame, three pictures, five names and a count

Everything in this section is inference from the two bodies of evidence. None of it has been tested on players.

### One frame, used everywhere, never flipped

Because expert patterns are tied to place and break when mirrored, the most important single decision is a frame that never changes: strength always runs the same way, the strong end is always in the same place, and the same landmarks appear on every chart. A workable choice is **strength running up the screen with the strongest hands at the top**. Then "the top of his range" and "everything above the line continues" are literally true of the picture; two players' strips stand side by side, which is the correct alignment for vertical pictures; a portrait phone gives the strength axis its long side; and the map is simply the strip opened sideways, with settled hands on one side and unsettled on the other, as the tool's own write-up already sketches it. Horizontal strips stacked one above the other satisfy the alignment research equally; the reason to prefer vertical is that the strip and the map then share an axis. If the existing strategy bar runs the other way, the frame it uses should be changed once and then frozen. The frame should carry two fixed landmarks: the 50% equity mark and the 70% mark.

### How each element should be drawn

Position should carry strength and nothing else. Length along the strip should carry share of the range, weighted by combinations, which removes the grid's area fault. The research on countable marks suggests drawing the strip as a fixed number of equal dots or blocks, each the same share of the range, so that "how often does it bet" is a count of coloured dots and the line is "after the eighth dot". Fifty dots is the count with confirmed evidence, and fewer will suit a phone; the right number is an open question. Colour should carry action only, as two or three flat colours, backed up by position so the chart still reads in grey. Frequency should never be a shade: a band that mixes should show its split as a count or a divided block, with a digit on it. The second position axis on the map carries how settled the hand is. Each chart gets a title that states its conclusion in words ("Defend: line at the 60% mark; draws come along"), since the title is what survives in memory, and labels sit on the bands, not in a legend: integrating words with the picture is one of the larger effects in the instructional literature (g = 0.63 across 58 comparisons; [Schroeder & Cenkci 2018](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/CHU_KR-23_Schroeder-2018_Lernmaterial.pdf)). Card art and decoration should stay out; irrelevant detail next to a diagram lowers learning ([Sundararajan & Adesope 2020](https://news.wsu.edu/press-release/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning)). On colour-blind-safe palettes and the best number of colours no learning evidence was found; treat those as ordinary design practice.

### The pictures and their jobs

Each view needs a stated job, or extra views only add translation work ([Ainsworth 2006](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/output/23577631/deft-a-conceptual-framework-for-considering-learning-with-multiple-representations)). The **line** is for facing a bet on any street: your strip alone, one cut (a second on the river for raises or bluff-catchers), sorted by equity plus draw on the flop, with the price drawn on the scale. The **three-band strip** is for river betting: bluff, check, value (74%; five bands reach 86%). The **two-by-three map** is for flop and turn betting. The **two strips side by side with the 70% ticks** are for the range-level question before betting: the gap between the two ticks is the landmark. The **grid** stays as the lookup by hand name, with tapping a hand lighting the same hand on the strip or map and the reverse; using the familiar view to guide the reading of the new one is the best-supported form of translation help. The **equity curve** stays as a detail view for defending, with a reference diagonal and the departure from it named.

### Three ideas from the literature that are not on the list

A **count with cut-offs**, in the backgammon manner: count the share of each range above 70% equity, take the difference, and learn two cut-offs that separate mostly check, mixed and mostly bet. The experiments found this the best single landmark (13.0 points off), so the cut-offs can be fitted from the tool's own data. It will be right on direction about seven times in ten; it should be taught as that, not as a frequency.

A **three-question tree drawn on the picture**. The "simple rule" in the tool's first experiment was already a rule three questions deep, so the tree exists; drawing each question as a glance at one part of the strips makes the tree and the chart the same object. The order of the questions sets which way the rule errs ([Luan et al. 2011](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_2099024_9/component/file_2099023/SLU_Signal_2011.pdf)), so it can be tuned to fail toward the cheaper mistake.

**Nested blocks for "of these, how many"**: on a band, show the betting share as a block inside the band, the layout that helped most in the probability studies.

### What the charts should not claim

Bet size. On 223 flops no picture got a simple rule closer than 14 to 15 points of pot, barely better than nothing. The coaching rule that nut advantage sets size ([vip-grinders](https://www.vip-grinders.com/poker-strategy/range-advantage/)) found no support in these measurements. That does not prove no such picture exists, only that none of these is it. Train mode's "biggest bet size" question should be taught from the written explanation until one is found.

## Teach the charts by prediction, drawing and sorting, not by display

The earlier report recommended prediction before the reveal, a come-back queue, contrast drills and fading explanations. The chart research says how each should work when the object is a picture.

**Predict by drawing.** Asking people to draw their expectation and then showing the gap improved recall and understanding, more for visual than text presentation ([Kim, Reinecke & Hullman 2017](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/explaining-the-gap)); whether the gain lasts beyond the session was not established. The line is the natural case: show the strip uncoloured, let the player drag the line, then show the solver's line and the gap. For betting spots, pick the shape name first. This also counters deceptive clarity.

**Draw from memory, then correct.** Drawing beats writing or viewing for later recall whatever the drawing skill, though the evidence is for single words ([Wammes et al. 2016](https://uwspace.uwaterloo.ca/items/a8b52734-18f3-4527-8be2-008a105c85f2)), and learner drawing beat controls in 26 of 28 comparisons for science text, median d = 0.40 ([review cited here](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1452385/pdf)). Drawing from memory without good correction was no better than drawing with the source open at two and eight weeks ([Kim et al. 2025](https://cris-portal.uni-muenster.de/portal/en/publication/171862949)), so the tool must always overlay the true chart. A frame plus one to three lines is drawable with a thumb; a grid is not. Novices need a partly built picture: give the frame and landmarks and ask only for the line, or leave one band blank. Fully built diagrams did as well as self-built ones in one set of studies ([Stull & Mayer 2007](https://escholarship.org/content/qt8x60f70g/qt8x60f70g.pdf?t=op2mr8)), so completion is the safer default.

**Explain first, then drill fast.** Linking two views is two skills, explaining the mapping and seeing it quickly, and in one tutoring study neither kind of support helped alone; the better-supported order is explanation first ([Rau and colleagues](https://website.education.wisc.edu/rau-lab/pubs/RauRummelAleven_ICLS2014_camera-ready_final_fixed.pdf)). After that, short classification drills on many varied examples improved later problem solving in mathematics, with effects of 0.14 to 0.48 in a classroom trial ([Kellman et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6124488); [Massey et al. poster](https://kellmanlab.psych.ucla.edu/files/massey_ies_pl_mtg_poster_2016.pdf)); the evidence is mostly from one laboratory. The drills here are "which of the five shapes is this strip?", "which board gives this shape?", "which grid matches this strip?" and "which of five positions is the line in?". Transfer in those studies came from matching across views, so the drills should cross views.

**Onboard the map, not the strip.** Onboarding measurably helped only for the least familiar chart type tested ([Stoiber et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11134)), and even that result is disputed. Introduce the strip as "the grid's hands, re-sorted by strength", with linked highlighting. Introduce the map later, by placing three or four hands every player knows (top set, an overpair, a flush draw, a missed hand) and asking the player to place two more. Begin each shape on a real board with the cards visible and fade to the bare outline and name; the fading sequence is the best-supported compromise but its evidence base is small ([Fyfe et al. 2014](https://pc.cogs.indiana.edu/?p=885)). Stagger the new charts: line first, river bands second, shapes third, map last.

**Feed the existing queue and contrast drills.** A missed shape or line position should return on a new board as an idea. Contrast pairs should be two strips on the same frame differing in one card. As skill grows, labels and explanations should collapse so the picture stands alone.

Train mode's four questions map onto this directly. "Range advantage" becomes the 70% tick gap. "Nut advantage" should be kept in the explanations but not sold as the frequency cue, since the 90% mark added nothing. "What a hand can do" becomes "where is this hand relative to the line" or "which region of the map".

## What is not known and needs real users

The central link is untested anywhere: no study found designs a chart for later mental picturing and checks whether people can reason from the remembered chart. Everything about "visualise it at the table" is extrapolated from chess, abacus and memory studies. The specific open questions, in rough order of importance, are these. Can players place the line without the tool, on boards they have not seen, after a gap of days? That is the test the earlier report's delayed-accuracy measure was built for. Do players who learn the charts decide better than players who study the grid for the same time? Can anyone hold the six-region map, or does it stay a study view? Do five shapes on one frame blur into each other over many spots (interference is a plausible risk the chess work only partly addresses)? Which orientation and how many dots work on a phone, for which no relevant evidence was found? Does the shape name erase the deviations that matter?

The tool's experiments have their own limits. They cover 132 flops and four pot types. Their cuts were optimised per decision. Their "simple rule" is a stand-in for a person, not a person. And 71% on direction is only eight points better than knowing the situation alone.

The cheapest first test costs nothing to build: show a weaker player a candidate chart with no caption and ask what it tells them. The owner is the person least able to judge this, for the reason the curse-of-knowledge study gives.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the complaint. The grid and the curve are not poor because they show too much or come from an older game; they are poor for study because the answer is not a visible thing on them. The tool's experiments then say something the literature could not: the answer has a different shape for different decisions. Defending is a line. River betting is three bands. Early-street betting is not ordered by strength at all, and needs a second idea, how settled the hand is. So the goal is not one better chart but one frame with a few pictures, each matched to a decision, and it is worth saying to users that a chart gives direction and membership, never frequency to the point or bet size.

The second implication is about what kind of evidence the project now has. The experiments establish that the answer is present in few pieces, which is necessary and is more than any poker product has published. Whether a person can learn, hold and use those pieces is a separate question that only the tool's users can answer, and the drills recommended here (drag the line, name the shape, redraw from memory) are also the measurements. Each produces a number per idea per player, so the same build that teaches the charts will show which of them deserve to stay.
