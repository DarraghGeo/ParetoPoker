Pareto training guide

Twelve spots,
one chart

How to decide whether to bet, check, call or fold after the flop, using one picture of the two ranges, four things to notice and two sums.

What this guide is

After the flop, every decision in a heads-up pot is one of twelve spots: flop, turn or river; first to act or facing a bet; in position or out of position. This guide gives one small table for each. Find your hand in the table and it tells you what to do.

The tables were made from 6731 decisions solved by a poker solver, and then tested on boards and matchups they had never seen. Every number in this guide is from those tests. Nothing here is opinion.

What it is not

It does not cover preflop play, bet sizing, or raising. It tells you bet or check, and call or fold. Those four choices are where most of the money is, and they are the place to start.

How to read this guide

You do not need to memorise twelve tables. Three ideas carry almost all of it.

  1. Facing a bet, compare your equity with the price. The price is what the call costs as a share of the pot you would win. If your hand wins more often than the price, calling makes money. On the river that is the entire rule. On earlier streets you add a cushion, and draws get a discount.
  2. First to act, most hands check. Bet your strongest hands, your best draws, and, on the river, your worst hands as bluffs. The hands in the middle check, because betting them gains nothing.
  3. Out of position, check more. On the flop, check everything. On the turn, check to a player who bet the flop. This costs almost nothing and removes the hardest decisions.

A plan for learning it

Learn the spots in the order they appear here. The first six (facing a bet) use one sum and are the most reliable. The river spots come first because nothing can change after them.

Words used

Range: all the hands a player can hold at this point, given how the hand has been played. Equity: how often your hand wins against his range if all the cards were dealt out with no more betting; a tie counts as half. In position: you act last on every street. Out of position: you act first. Solver: a program that works out play that cannot be beaten; this guide treats it as the answer key. Big blind: the unit all pots and stacks are counted in.

In the tables, each cell says what to do, with a small number: how often the solver does it with those hands. Pale cells are rare.

Colours in the tables: bet or call check or fold either: the two are worth the same. The word is always written in the cell as well.

The chart

The chart puts both players' ranges side by side, sorted into eight rows by how strong the hand is right now.

Out of position is always on the left, in position on the right. A bar's length is how much of that player's range is in the row. The strongest row is at the top.

This example is a 3-bet pot on 8♦A♥Q♦. The player in position has been checked to. The coloured part of each bar is how much of that row the solver bets.

What it shows at a glance:

  • Who has the strong hands. Compare the top four rows on each side.
  • Who has the weak hands. Compare the bottom rows. This matters for bluffing.
  • Where your hand sits. Find its row, then ask whether it is a strong or weak example of that row.

From the row to equity

The row is a first guess at your equity; most tables ask for the equity itself, in wide bands. In the app the chart places each hand by its equity. Away from it, two rules of thumb get you into the right band:

  • On the river, your equity is the share of his range that you beat. If you beat about a third of the hands he can have, your equity is about 33%.
  • On the flop and turn, start from the share of his range you beat now, and add about 2 points for each card that would improve you to the best hand, per street still to come. A flush draw has nine such cards: about 18 points on the turn.

These rules of thumb were not tested. The results assume equity is known, as the app shows it.

Two sums and four reads

Sum 1: the price of a call

Divide what you must put in by the pot you would win, counting your own call.

Price = call ÷ (pot with his bet in it + call)

He betsPrice
A third of the pot20%
Half the pot25%
Two thirds29%
Three quarters30%
The pot33%
One and a quarter pots36%
Twice the pot40%

Example: the pot is 10 and he bets 7.5. You call 7.5 to win a pot of 25. Price: 7.5 ÷ 25 = 30%.

Sum 2: your equity against the price

Take your equity against the range he bets with, and subtract the price. The tables call this "over the price" or "under the price". Equity 42% against a price of 30% is 12 over.

Read 1: stack to pot

The smaller remaining stack divided by the pot. Three bands: under 2, 2 to 6, over 6. Pots that were raised twice or more before the flop are usually under 2.

Read 2: your draw

Flush draw: four to a flush using one of your cards. Open-ended: eight cards complete your straight. Gutshot: four cards do. A hand can be a pair and a draw at once; the draw still counts.

Read 3: who bet on the street before

You, him, or nobody. On the flop, it is whoever made the last raise before the flop.

Read 4: his weak hands

How much of his range has less than 30% equity against yours. On the chart, it is the bottom of his side. Three bands: under a fifth of his range, a fifth to 45%, over 45%.

That is everything the twelve tables use: the chart row, your equity, two sums and four reads. No table uses more than two of them at once.

Spot 3Turn, facing a bet, out of position

Look at

  • The price of the call (sum 1).
  • Your equity against his betting range.
  • Stack to pot (read 1).

In short

  • 15 points or more over the price: call.
  • 5 to 15 over: call only when stacks are short (under 2 pots behind).
  • Anything less: fold.

Why. Equity on the turn is not money yet. Out of position you act first on the river and he can bet again, so a hand just over the price often has to fold later and never collects. It needs a cushion. When stacks are short there is little left to bet, so the cushion can be smaller.

Spot 4Turn, facing a bet, in position

Look at

  • The price of the call (sum 1).
  • Your equity against his betting range.
  • Your draw (read 2).

In short

  • No draw: call with 5 points or more over the price.
  • Gutshot: call when over the price at all.
  • Flush draw: call unless you are 5 or more under.
  • Open-ended straight draw: call.

Why. A draw is worth more than its equity says: when it comes in you win a further bet, and when it misses you lose nothing more. In position you also see what he does on the river before you act. So draws may call slightly under the price, and made hands need a small cushion.

Spot 5Flop, facing a bet, out of position

Look at

  • The price of the call (sum 1).
  • Your equity against his betting range.
  • Your draw (read 2).

These spots are harder than facing a bet, and the tables are less exact here: they lose 0.1 to 0.8% of the pot per decision. The river in position is the one that costs most and repays study most.

Spot 7River, checked to you, in position

Look at

  • Your equity against his range.
  • How much of his range is weak (read 4).

In short

  • 80% equity or more: bet.
  • 65 to 80%: bet only when he has few weak hands (under a fifth of his range), because then he has hands that can call.
  • Under 20% equity: bluff, unless he has few weak hands.
  • 20 to 50%: check, unless nearly half his range is weak.
  • 50 to 65%: always check. These hands win at showdown and gain nothing by betting.

Why. A river bet makes money in two ways: worse hands call (value) or better hands fold (a bluff). Hands in the middle do neither, so they check. How far down value goes, and how many bluffs you may add, both depend on what he holds: value needs him to have hands that call, bluffs need him to have hands that fold.

Spot 8River, first to act, out of position

Look at

  • Your equity against his range.
  • How much of his range is weak (read 4).

In short

  • He has few weak hands (under a fifth): bet with 65% equity or more, check the rest.
  • He has many weak hands (over 45%): bet only your worst hands as bluffs, check everything else, strong hands included.
  • In between: check everything.

Why. Out of position a check is not the end: he may bet, and then your strong hands collect anyway. So betting for value pays only when his range is strong enough to call. When his range is weak he cannot call, so strong hands check and let him bluff, and your hopeless hands take the pot by betting.

Spot 9Turn, checked to you, in position

Look at

  • Your equity against his range.
  • Your draw (read 2).

In short

  • 80% equity or more: bet.
  • A flush draw or an open-ended straight draw: bet.
  • Everything else checks, gutshots and medium-strength hands included.

Why. Strong hands bet to build the pot. Good draws bet because they can win at once when he folds and can still make the best hand when he calls. Medium hands have the most to lose from being raised, so they check and see the river for free.

Look at

In short

  • He bet the flop and you called: check everything.
  • You bet the flop and he called: bet overpairs and draws. Check the rest.

Why. The player who bet last usually holds the stronger range, and betting into a stronger range from out of position loses money. Checking the whole range to the flop bettor costs almost nothing. How to read these numbers

  • A loss of 0.3% of the pot per decision is small: in a pot of 20 big blinds it is 0.06 of a big blind.
  • The two columns of naive play show how much there is to win. Where "always check or fold" is already low (spots 10 and 12), the decision barely matters. Where both naive columns are high (spot 7 above all), skill pays.
  • "Worst tenth" is the loss on the worst 10% of decisions. For spot 7 it is nearly 2% of the pot: the table is a start there, not the last word.

What to keep in mind

  • The opponent is the solver. The tables are the best simple reply to solver play. Against a player who adapts, always taking the same action with the same kind of hand can be read. The solver mixes many of these hands; the small number in each cell shows how often.
  • Equity is assumed known. The tests used exact equity. Your estimate will be rougher. Most hands are far from the lines, so wide bands forgive a lot; the hands near a line cost little either way.
  • The flop tables rest on fewer decisions (41 to 80 each, against hundreds or more for the turn and river). Treat them as less certain.
  • One game. Six-handed cash, 100 big blinds deep, two players after the flop. Other stack depths were not tested.
  • Bet size and raises are not covered. As a default, bet a third of the pot on the flop and three quarters on the turn and river.

Appendix: exact rules, for building an engine

This section restates the guide with no room for interpretation. An engine that follows it should reproduce the losses in the results table on spots it has not seen, when measured the same way.

Scope

No-limit hold'em, two players after the flop, six-handed cash ranges, 100 big blinds effective. Decision nodes: the first decision of each player on a street (first to act, or checked to) and facing a bet or raise. Output: BET or CHECK when no bet is faced; CALL or FOLD when one is.

Inputs at a decision

Board (3 to 5 cards); hero's two cards; hero's range and villain's range at this node as weights per two-card combination, after everything that has happened, including villain's bet when one is faced; the pot including all bets; the amount to call; the effective stack behind; the betting history.

Definitions

Equity of a hand: its expected share of the pot against villain's range at this node if the remaining board cards are dealt with no further betting, weighting villain's combinations by range weight, removing combinations that share a card with hero's hand or the board. A tie counts as half.

Equity bands: under 0.20; 0.20 to under 0.35; 0.35 to under 0.50; 0.50 to under 0.65; 0.65 to under 0.80; 0.80 or more.

Price = to call ÷ (pot including all bets + to call). Margin = equity − price. Margin bands: under −0.05 ("5+ under the price"); −0.05 to under 0 ("0-5 under"); 0 to under 0.05 ("0-5 over"); 0.05 to under 0.15 ("5-15 over"); 0.15 or more ("15+ over").

Row, the first that applies. "Board ranks" are the distinct ranks on the board, highest first.

  1. Trips or better: five cards of a suit among hole and board cards with at least one hole card of that suit and fewer than five of it on the board; or the best straight from hole and board cards is higher than the best straight on the board alone; or a hole card's rank appears three or more times among hole and board cards.
  2. Two pair: the hole cards are of different ranks and each matches a board rank.
  3. Pocket pair: higher than the top board rank: overpair. Otherwise, at least as high as the second board rank: middle pair. Otherwise: low pair.
  4. One hole card matches a board rank: the top board rank: top pair. The second: middle pair. Otherwise: low pair.
  5. Draw (flop and turn only): exactly four cards of a suit among hole and board cards including a hole card; or at least two different ranks would each give a higher straight than the hand has now.
  6. Air: anything else.

Draw class, for any hand including made hands, flop and turn only (on the river: no draw). Flush draw: nine cards complete a flush that uses a hole card. Otherwise open-ended: eight or more cards complete a straight. Otherwise gutshot: four to seven cards do. Otherwise no draw.

Stack to pot = effective stack behind ÷ pot including all bets. Bands: under 2; 2 to under 6; 6 or more.

His weak hands = the share, by range weight, of villain's range whose equity against hero's range is under 0.30. Bands: under 0.20; 0.20 to under 0.45; 0.45 or more.

Who bet on the street before: the last player to bet or raise on the previous street: "I did", "he did", or "nobody" if it was checked through. On the flop, the last player to raise before the flop.

Procedure

Identify the spot from the street, hero's seat and whether a bet is faced. Compute that spot's inputs. Look the cell up below. Cells marked "either is fine" may take either action; the action written is the one that lost less. Cells marked "did not occur; filled" had no hands in the data and take the neighbouring band's action.

How to check an engine

For each decision in a set of solved spots: for each hand in hero's range, take the solver's value of the best action and of the engine's action, and sum the difference over the range by weight, divided by the pot. Where the engine bets and several bet sizes exist, value the bet as the solver's own mix of sizes for that hand, or the best size if the solver never bets it; likewise for calling against raising. Average over decisions. Solves used: bet sizes of 33%, 75% and 125% of the pot on the flop and 75% on the turn and river, raises to three times the bet, solved to within 0.3% of the pot.
