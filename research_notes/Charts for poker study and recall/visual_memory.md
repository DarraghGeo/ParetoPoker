# Visual memory, mental imagery and expert chunking: what makes a visual representation recallable and usable from memory

Method note for the report writer: in this session full-text fetching of the main hosts (PMC, Harvard VCG) was blocked by the network policy, so nearly every finding below was verified against abstracts, repository records and search excerpts, not full texts. Where a number comes only from a press release or secondary summary it is marked "(secondary)". Items I know from background knowledge but could not confirm against a source in this session are placed under Gaps, not Cited Findings.

## 1. Capacity limits: visual working memory, mental imagery, spatial vs object imagery

### Takeaway
A person can hold roughly 3-4 visual "objects" or chunks at once, and a mental image is weaker still: its quality drops as soon as more than one item is imagined, and images are hard to reinterpret or "read off" in ways not planned at encoding. Capacity is raised not by adding slots but by structure (grouping, regularity, known patterns), and spatial layout is a more robust and more widely shared imagery code than pictorial detail such as colour.

### Cited Findings
- Luck & Vogel (1997, Nature 390, 279-281) introduced the change-detection array paradigm; the capacity limit they found stays roughly constant regardless of how many features each object carries (i.e. the unit is the integrated object, not the feature). — [Journal of Cognition review citing Luck & Vogel](https://journalofcognition.org/articles/387)
- Cowan (2001, Behavioral and Brain Sciences 24, 87-114) re-evaluated Miller's 7±2 and argued the typical limit is about 4 chunks (3-4 when rehearsal and chunking are prevented); whether a fixed item limit exists is still disputed by resource-model researchers. — [Cowan 2001 PDF](https://memory.psych.missouri.edu/assets/doc/articles/2001/cowan-bbs-2001.pdf)
- Brady, Konkle & Alvarez (2011, Journal of Vision 11(5):4) review: models of visual memory must account for what is stored, not just how many items; capacity is better described as number of items times fidelity per item, and memory uses structured representations (ensembles, hierarchies, prior knowledge) rather than independent items. — [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21617025/); [open PDF](https://bradylab.ucsd.edu/pdfs/BradyKonkleAlvarez2011.pdf)
- Brady, Konkle & Alvarez (2009, JEP: General 138, 487-502): when colours in a display co-occurred predictably, observers remembered more of them; performance improved as the regularities were learned, as a Bayesian learner predicts; the authors conclude capacity is fixed in information terms and regular input is stored in compressed form. — [Harvard DASH abstract](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/a9b32c42-474e-4d98-83cb-9213f6bccce7)
- Contested: Ngiam, Brissenden & Awh (2019) replicated the compression benefit but only in participants who could explicitly recall the colour pairs afterwards, arguing the gain comes from long-term memory retrieval rather than more efficient working-memory storage. Either way, the practical result holds: learned regularities let more be reported. — [Ngiam et al. preprint](https://williamngiam.github.io/files/Ngiam,%20Brissenden%20and%20Awh%20(2019)%20Preprint.pdf)
- Perceptual grouping raises what can be held: Woodman, Vecera & Luck (2003) found that connecting two stimuli improved change-detection accuracy by about 6% and grouping by proximity by about 12% at set size 6 (figures as reported in a later review). — [Review citing Woodman et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4068940); [paper record](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Perceptual-organization-influences-visual-working-memory/9984066383402771)
- Items are stored relative to the overall configuration: Jiang, Olson & Chun (2000) found retrieval of an item is worse when the surrounding spatial context changes between study and test. — [Review citing Jiang et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3806891)
- Symmetry helps recall of spatial patterns, but only when the symmetry can actually be detected (all three symmetry types tested improved recall when targets were shown simultaneously). — [Gestalt effects in VWM study](https://core.ac.uk/download/53131207.pdf)
- Mental imagery capacity: Keogh & Pearson (2017, Cognition 162, 124-132), using a binocular-rivalry priming measure that does not rely on self-report, conclude that imagery "is severely limited by the perceptual and phenomenal bottleneck of visual representation"; imagery strength/precision falls as set size rises, with the largest drop from one to two items (the one-to-two detail is secondary). They also note that very little prior research had measured imagery capacity. — [Paper mirror](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/cognitive-bias/illusion-of-depth/2017-keogh.pdf); [BPS Research Digest summary](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/scientific-measure-our-visual-imagination-suggests-it-surprisingly-limited)
- Limits on reinterpreting images: Chambers & Reisberg (1985, JEP: HPP 11, 317) found people could not reverse classic ambiguous figures (duck/rabbit, Necker cube, Schroeder staircase) in a mental image, although they could once they drew the image. — [Peterson et al. 1992](https://ocf.berkeley.edu/~jfkihlstrom/PDFs/1990s/1992/Peterson_etal1992.pdf)
- Qualification: Finke, Pinker & Farah (1989, Cognitive Science 13, 51-78) showed that people can assign novel interpretations to images they assemble from simple parts or mentally transform; they suggest duck/rabbit-type reversals need low-level global perceptual processes that imagery cannot access. — [Pinker publication page](https://stevenpinker.com/publications/reinterpreting-visual-patterns-mental-imagery)
- Peterson, Kihlstrom, Rose & Glisky (1992, Memory & Cognition): reference-frame realignments of an image can occur, particularly with an explicit or implicit hint; "reconstruals" within the same frame are common. Summary verdict in the literature on whether images can be reinterpreted: "It depends". — [Peterson et al. 1992](https://ocf.berkeley.edu/~jfkihlstrom/PDFs/1990s/1992/Peterson_etal1992.pdf); [Brandimonte & Gerbino 1993](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03211161.pdf)
- Aphantasia prevalence: Dance, Ipser & Simner (2022, Consciousness and Cognition 97) estimate 3.9% of the general population have absent or dim imagery on the VVIQ (0.8% fully absent), from over 1,000 people in two samples; earlier estimates ranged from 0.7% to 15.3% depending on cut-off. — [Dance et al. accepted manuscript](https://sro.sussex.ac.uk/id/eprint/103365/1/SIMNER_Consciousness_and_Cognition_NOV_2021.pdf)
- Zeman et al. (2020) figures as cited by later papers: 0.7% extreme aphantasia (VVIQ 16/80), 2.6% moderate aphantasia, 11.2% moderate hyperphantasia. A 2024 international sample (n = 3,049) gave 1.2% aphantasia, 3% hypophantasia, 89.9% typical, 5.9% hyperphantasia. The estimates are cut-off dependent. — [Frontiers 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1174873/pdf); [Frontiers 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1454107/full)
- Object vs spatial imagery are dissociable: Kozhevnikov, Kosslyn & Shephard (2005, Memory & Cognition 33, 710-726) identified "object visualizers" (good at pictorial detail, poor at spatial tasks; process images holistically) and "spatial visualizers" (good at mental rotation and paper folding; process part by part). — [Springer article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03195337)
- Aphantasia spares spatial memory: Bainbridge et al. (2021, Cortex), 61 aphantasic participants drawing scenes from memory, scored by 2,795 online raters: aphantasics recalled significantly fewer objects and less colour and relied more on verbal labels, but their spatial accuracy was equal to controls and they made fewer memory errors. — [Westminster repository record](https://westminsterresearch.westminster.ac.uk/item/v2475/quantifying-aphantasia-through-drawing-those-without-visual-imagery-show-deficits-in-object-but-not-spatial-memory); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7856239)

### Inferences
- A 13x13 grid of 169 independently coloured cells is roughly 40 times the 3-4 item limit; it can only be held if it collapses into a handful of chunks (regions with a simple boundary), which is what the compression and grouping findings predict.
- A recallable study chart should be describable as about 3-4 parts on a fixed frame, each part a simple shape. More detail must be reachable by "zooming" into one part at a time, not held simultaneously.
- Because images are hard to reinterpret after the fact (Chambers & Reisberg), the question a chart must answer at the table should be built into how it is encoded (the boundary line is the answer), rather than expecting the player to picture the chart and then derive something new from it. Simple part-based images are the exception where novel read-offs work (Finke et al.).
- Colour and fine pictorial detail belong to the weaker, less universal "object" imagery code; position, extent and shape belong to the spatial code that is preserved even in people with no pictorial imagery. Designs should carry the meaning in position and shape, with colour as redundant reinforcement only.
- Some 1-4% of users cannot form pictorial images at all; a design relying on spatial structure plus a verbal label still works for them.

### Gaps
- Kosslyn's own image-maintenance findings (images fade within about a second unless refreshed; more parts take longer to generate and are harder to hold) are well known but I did not find a primary source in this session; treat as background, unverified.
- Exact set-size numbers in Keogh & Pearson (2017) were not retrieved.
- No study was found measuring how many regions of a coloured matrix or heat map people can reproduce from memory; the 3-4 figure is extrapolated from array studies with simple objects.
- Whether aphantasics perform normally on mental rotation was not confirmed in this session.

## 2. Long-term visual memory and what makes images or charts memorable

### Takeaway
Long-term memory for meaningful, distinct pictures is enormous, but for abstract charts the evidence says people keep the gist and the title, not the data; what makes a chart recognisable (colour, pictures, unusual form) is not the same as what lets someone recall its content (title, text, redundancy, relevance). Memory for graph shape is systematically distorted toward schematic norms (symmetry, the 45° diagonal).

### Cited Findings
- Brady, Konkle, Alvarez & Oliva (2008, PNAS 105, 14325-14329): after viewing 2,500 real-world objects over 5.5 hours, two-alternative recognition was 92% against a novel-category foil, 88% against a same-category foil and 87% against the same object in a different state. The authors treat this as a lower bound. Stimuli were meaningful, nameable objects. — [PMC full text record](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2533687); [MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2008/vision-memory-0908)
- Dual coding (Paivio): the mind processes verbal and non-verbal information on two channels, and pictures tend to be remembered better than words because they can be coded both ways; the theory is not universally accepted and alternative explanations of picture superiority exist. — [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy supplement](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2014/entries/mental-imagery/dual-coding-theory.html)
- Borkin et al. (2013, IEEE TVCG / InfoVis), 2,070 single-panel visualisations: colour and the inclusion of a human-recognisable object (photographs, body parts, icons) enhanced memorability; common graph types were less memorable than unique visualisation types. — [Harvard VCG publication page](https://vcg.seas.harvard.edu/publications/20130101-what-makes-a-visualization-memorable)
- Colour count in Borkin 2013 (secondary): visualisations with more than six (reported elsewhere as seven or more) colours were more memorable than those with few colours or a single-hue gradient; the two secondary sources differ slightly on the threshold. — [Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/3021394/the-secrets-of-a-memorable-infographic); [Tableau blog](https://www.tableau.com/blog/how-get-people-remember-your-visualization)
- Important limit: the 2013 measure was recognition ("have I seen this image before?") after a brief exposure, not recall of content or ability to use it. This is why the follow-up study was run. — [Harvard SEAS news](https://seas.harvard.edu/news/making-visualizations-more-memorable)
- Borkin et al. (2016, IEEE TVCG 22; "Beyond Memorability: Visualization Recognition and Recall"), combining eye tracking, recognition and written recall: titles and supporting text are the key elements people fixate and later recall, and should carry the message; pictograms did not hurt and, used appropriately, improved recall; redundancy (saying the message in more than one form) helped; visualisations memorable at a glance also tended to have their message recalled. — [Harvard VCG publication page](https://vcg.seas.harvard.edu/publications/20150101-beyond-memorability-visualization-recognition-and-recall); [Storybench interview with Borkin](https://www.storybench.org/?p=3569)
- Caveat from Borkin (secondary): clutter and pictograms unrelated to the content make people slower; "that's not effective design". — [Storybench](https://www.storybench.org/?p=3569)
- Bateman et al. (2010, CHI; "Useful Junk?"): for Holmes-style embellished charts versus plain versions, interpretation accuracy was no worse and recall after a two-to-three-week gap was significantly better; the sample was small (20 participants, 10 immediate and 10 delayed). — [Bateman et al. PDF](https://vis.csail.mit.edu/classes/6.859/readings/pdfs/Bateman-UsefulJunk.pdf)
- Contested: Stephen Few's critique argues the study's small sample, unrepresentative hand-drawn embellishments and simple charts do not justify general conclusions, and that the benefit appeared only on the delayed test. — [Few 2011 critique](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~telea001/uploads/VACourse/Few11.pdf)
- Relevance moderates embellishment: a follow-up study found data-relevant enhancements helped recall of the title and theme but did not significantly affect recall of specific data values, and unrelated embellishments produced worse topic recall than plain charts. — [Memorability of Enhanced Informational Graphics](https://journals.shareok.org/ijsw/article/view/54)
- Gist distortion in graph memory: Tversky & Schiano (1989, JEP: General 118, 387-398) found nearly symmetric curves were remembered as more symmetric, a verbal description suggesting symmetry or asymmetry shifted memory in that direction, and lines in graphs (but not in maps) were remembered as closer to the 45° diagonal. — [Record](https://opact.sdu.edu.tr/vufind/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-a391t-a895675e27ba08337c115609670181625704ad80223f08e5c98b6fe68567ef13)
- Schiano & Tversky (1992, Memory & Cognition): the 45° bias appeared only when the lines were interpreted as graphs; the same lines uninterpreted were biased away from the axes and diagonal. Memory for a graph is anchored to a conceptual reference frame, not stored as a picture. — [Springer article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03208249)

### Inferences
- The "minimalism versus embellishment" debate is partly a confusion of outcomes: embellishment and colour raise recognition of the image; recall of the message depends on title, text and relevance; neither Borkin nor Bateman shows that people can reproduce data values or shapes from memory. No study in this group tested reasoning from a remembered chart.
- Borkin's "more colours are more memorable" finding concerns telling images apart in a recognition test and should not be read as "use many colours in a chart meant to be pictured"; working-memory and imagery limits (section 1) point the other way for recall.
- A study chart should carry a short verbal title that states its conclusion, since that is what survives.
- Because graph memory regresses toward norms (symmetry, 45°), a recallable chart should make its informative feature a deviation from a named reference line (e.g. "bulges above the diagonal on the left"), so the norm does the storage and only the deviation has to be remembered. Equity distribution curves fit this: remember the reference diagonal plus one or two named departures.
- Distinctiveness matters for retrieval: a family of charts that all look the same (169-cell grids differing in a few cells) gives no distinct cue per chart, which the "common graphs are least memorable" result is consistent with.

### Gaps
- Borkin 2013 details I recall but could not verify in this session (host blocked): about 261 Mechanical Turk participants, 410 target images, high between-observer consistency, and that low data-ink ratio and high visual density were associated with higher memorability. Verify before quoting.
- Borkin 2016 participant numbers and exposure times were not retrieved.
- No peer-reviewed study was found on memory for heat maps or matrix charts specifically.
- Shepard (1967) and Standing (1973) on picture recognition capacity, and the size of the picture superiority effect, were not sourced in this session.

## 3. Expert memory in games: chunks, templates and blindfold play

### Takeaway
Game experts do not have better visual memory; they recognise a large learned vocabulary of location-specific patterns tied to the fixed board, and for common position types they hold "templates" (a fixed core plus slots for variable details). Blindfold play shows the representation is visuospatial but schematic and relational: it is the board frame and the pieces' functional relations that are held, not a picture.

### Cited Findings
- Chase & Simon (1973): after a 5-second view, masters reconstructed game positions far better than weaker players, but with randomly placed pieces all players, master to novice, recalled only about three or four pieces; the advantage "cannot be explained in terms of any general superiority of memory". — [Chase & Simon 1973 PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/chess/1973-simon.pdf)
- Refinement: Gobet & Simon (1996) and later re-analyses found a small but statistically reliable skill effect even for random positions at short presentation times (10 seconds or less), because random positions still contain some familiar small patterns by chance. — [Gobet & Simon, random positions](https://core.ac.uk/download/333524.pdf)
- Gobet & Simon (1996, Memory & Cognition): recall is impaired when positions are mirror-reflected, implying "each chunk represents a specific pattern of pieces in a specific location"; simulation supports the estimate that masters hold some 50,000 chunks. — [Springer article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03200937)
- Template theory (Gobet & Simon 1996, Cognitive Psychology 31, "Templates in Chess Memory: A Mechanism for Recalling Several Boards"): masters can recall several boards shown in quick succession, exceeding what short-term memory chunks allow; they use long-term retrieval structures (templates) consisting of core information plus slots where variable information, perceptual or abstract, can be filled in quickly. — [Paper record](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/templates-in-chess-memory-a-mechanism-for-recalling-several-boards/812454498524987392-11891); [Gobet 1998 review](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/815/1/FullText.pdf)
- Go: Reitman (1976, Cognitive Psychology 8, 336-356) applied the chunk-timing method to a Go master and a beginner; no single pause threshold produced consistent chunks, and the master described the patterns as overlapping clusters, not a linear list or strict hierarchy. — [Paper summary](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/skilled-perception-in-go-deducing-memory-structures-from-inter-response-times/812340189845258240-11891)
- Bridge: Engle & Bukstel (1978) and Charness (1979) replicated the expert advantage for meaningful (structured) card material in bridge players. — [Gobet 1998 review](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/815/1/FullText.pdf)
- Blindfold chess relies mainly on visuospatial working memory and makes little use of verbal working memory (Saariluoma's interference experiments, as reviewed by Campitelli & Gobet); Saariluoma & Kalakoski (1997) are cited as confirming that chess memory has a significant visuospatial component and that mental imagery matters in play. — [Campitelli & Gobet 2005](https://core.ac.uk/download/333506.pdf); [Mental imagery and chunks](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/1618/1/Mental%20imagery%20and%20chunks.pdf)
- Saariluoma (1992) dual-task design (8 masters above 2200 Elo, 8 intermediates below 1750): a concurrent visuospatial task impaired chess problem solving more than a verbal one (secondary; the journal reference for this paper is given inconsistently across sources). — [Science-of-chess summary](https://lichess.org/@/NDpatzer/blog/science-of-chess-how-does-chess-calculation-depend-on-words-vs-pictures/gBpG2olI)
- Campitelli & Gobet (2005, European Journal of Cognitive Psychology 17, 23-45): masters following a game presented blindfold-style were disrupted by irrelevant background information only when that information changed during presentation; static irrelevant context was filtered out. The authors conclude the template theory explains most blindfold data. — [Campitelli & Gobet 2005](https://core.ac.uk/download/333506.pdf)

### Inferences
- The transferable design lesson is the pairing of (a) one fixed spatial frame that never changes, with (b) a small vocabulary of recurring patterns that always sit in the same place on it. Expert memory is location-specific (mirror-image result), so a study chart must always use the same orientation and axes; flipping or re-sorting it between views destroys the chunks.
- Templates suggest a two-level format: a few canonical "position type" skeletons learned once, and each new chart encoded as "skeleton X with these two or three slot values changed". This is how experts hold several boards at once and is the best available model for holding several ranges at once.
- Chunks in games are defined by functional relations (attack, defence, proximity), not just adjacency. A chart region should therefore correspond to a strategic meaning (a class of hands that plays the same way for the same reason), not an arbitrary colour band.
- Go's overlapping clusters warn that a clean partition is not required and may not be natural; what matters is that each cluster is familiar.
- The 13x13 grid is itself a fixed frame with familiar anchors (pairs diagonal, suited above, offsuit below), which is a genuine strength to keep; the weakness is the cell-by-cell colouring, not the frame.

### Gaps
- De Groot's original work (Thought and Choice in Chess) was not sourced directly; Chase & Simon's replication stands in for it.
- I did not find the template paper's specifics on template size (I recall cores of roughly ten or more pieces) or the amount of practice needed to acquire them; unverified.
- No peer-reviewed study of poker players' memory for ranges or charts was found; all poker implications are extrapolation from chess, Go and bridge.
- Details of what blindfold players report (e.g. that they do not see piece shapes or colours, only "lines of force") come from older introspective literature (Binet) which I did not source here.

## 4. Mnemonics and spatial frameworks: loci, pegs, schemas

### Takeaway
A stable, well-learned spatial scaffold is the single most effective known device for exact recall: ordinary people roughly double word-list recall after six weeks of loci training, and memory athletes' advantage comes from this strategy rather than from exceptional brains. Schema research adds that once a consistent skeleton exists, new instances that fit it are learned in one exposure and consolidated quickly.

### Cited Findings
- Dresler et al. (2017, Neuron): memory athletes recalled about 70.8 of 72 words versus about 39.9 for matched controls; 51 naive participants were assigned to six weeks of daily method-of-loci training, working-memory training or no training; the loci group gained about 35 words, versus about 11 for working-memory training and about 7 for no training, and the gain largely persisted at a four-month retest without further practice (numbers from press summaries of the paper). — [PMC record](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5439266); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/03/170308131236.htm)
- Maguire, Valentine, Wilding & Kapur (2003, Nature Neuroscience 6, 90-95): superior memorisers showed no exceptional intellectual ability or structural brain differences; nearly all (reported as nine of ten) used the method of loci, and during encoding they engaged regions for spatial memory and navigation (medial parietal, retrosplenial cortex, right posterior hippocampus). — [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12483214/)
- Meta-analysis: Twomey & Kroneisen (2021, QJEP) pooled 13 randomised trials of the method of loci and found a medium effect, g = 0.65 (95% CI 0.45-0.85; I² = 45.5%), with risk-of-bias reporting weak in most studies. — [Record](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/cmedm,33535926)
- A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis (British Journal of Psychology) found a large effect on immediate serial recall versus rehearsal (d = 0.88, 95% CI 0.47-1.25) but rated the evidence low to very low quality due to risk of bias. — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12514325/)
- Tse et al. (2007, Science, "Schemas and memory consolidation"): rats that had built up a schema of flavour-place associations in a familiar arena learned new associations in a single trial, and those new traces became independent of the hippocampus rapidly; schemas played a causal role in one-trial learning. — [Dundee record](https://discovery.dundee.ac.uk/en/publications/schemas-and-memory-consolidation/)
- van Kesteren et al. (2012, Trends in Neurosciences; SLIMM framework): information congruent with an existing schema is encoded and consolidated faster through medial prefrontal processing; information strongly incongruent with a schema can also be well remembered, as a novel episodic event; the in-between (weakly related) case is remembered worst. — [van Kesteren et al. 2012 PDF](https://www.mrc-cbu.cam.ac.uk/personal/rik.henson/personal/VanKesterenEtAl_12_TINS_schema_novelty_memory.pdf)

### Inferences
- The loci method and expert board memory share one mechanism: a fixed set of locations learned to automaticity, to which variable content is attached. A study chart with a canonical layout is a loci system for free, provided the layout never varies between charts.
- Schema findings predict a learning curve: the first few charts in a family are expensive; after the skeleton is established, each new chart costs little if it is presented as a variation of the skeleton, and the one feature that violates the skeleton will stand out and be remembered.
- The loci literature measures exact recall of lists, not reasoning. It supports "the frame aids retrieval" but not "the player can then compute with what is retrieved".
- Loci work best with a modest number of distinct stations; a 13x13 grid has too many equivalent cells to serve as stations, but its landmarks (the pairs diagonal, corners, the suited/offsuit halves, a few named rows) can.

### Gaps
- Bartlett (1932) on schematic reconstruction (memory normalises toward the schema) was not sourced in this session; the Tversky & Schiano graph result in section 2 is an example of the same effect.
- Peg systems: no peer-reviewed quantitative source retrieved.
- Exact pre/post values from the Dresler paper were not confirmed against the full text; secondary sources disagree on baseline (about 26-30 versus about 40 words).
- No study found on how stable a layout must be, or the cost of changing a learned layout, beyond the chess mirror-image result.

## 5. Generating versus viewing: drawing and redrawing from memory

### Takeaway
Drawing an item beats writing, viewing or imagining it for later free recall, robustly and regardless of drawing skill, and learner-generated drawing improves learning from text with a moderate average effect. Direct evidence that redrawing a diagram from memory (as opposed to drawing with the source open) improves later use is thin and mixed.

### Cited Findings
- Wammes, Meade & Fernandes (2016, QJEP 69, 1752-1776), seven free-recall experiments: drawn words were recalled better than written words; the advantage was not accounted for by deeper processing, visual imagery or viewing pictures, alone or together; it held with only a few seconds of drawing time and longer lists, and in a between-participants pure-list design (so it is not just relative distinctiveness). Proposed mechanism: integration of semantic, visual and motor information in one trace. Limitation: single words only. — [University of Waterloo repository](https://uwspace.uwaterloo.ca/items/a8b52734-18f3-4527-8be2-008a105c85f2)
- Drawing quality did not matter, and the benefit held with as little as 4 seconds to draw (secondary). — [University of Waterloo news](https://uwaterloo.ca/news/news/need-remember-something-better-draw-it-study-finds)
- Fernandes, Wammes & Meade (2018, Current Directions in Psychological Science 27, 302-308): gains from drawing exceed those from semantic elaboration, visualisation, writing and even tracing; benefits extend to older adults. — [Article record](https://www.deepdyve.com/lp/sage/the-surprisingly-powerful-influence-of-drawing-on-memory-u93uinbWY4)
- Generation versus copying: a dissertation study found drawing helped across conditions but more when drawings were self-generated than copied, and the effect weakened when drawing was used very frequently (unpublished dissertation; word recall). — [USM dissertation](https://aquila.usm.edu/dissertations/2347)
- Learner-generated drawing in instruction: in Fiorella & Mayer's (2015) review, drawing beat control conditions in 26 of 28 comparisons, median effect d = 0.40, mostly for learning from printed science text. — [Frontiers 2024 paper citing the review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1452385/pdf)
- Van Meter & Garner (2005) is the foundational review of learner-generated drawing; a later systematic meta-analysis covers work since then, and meta-analyses by Fiorella & Zhang (2018) and Cromley, Du & Dane (2019) exist. — [Drawing-to-learn meta-analysis record](https://www.scinapse.io/papers/2995273720); [Nottingham repository paper](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/index.php/OutputFile/5808285)
- Support for drawing: a 2025 meta-analysis of 14 studies found drawing support in general was not related to learning outcomes; an earlier meta-analysis likewise found supported drawing did not outperform unsupported drawing. — [Educational Psychology Review 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-025-10067-7)
- Drawing from memory versus with the source open: Kim et al. (2025, Learning and Instruction) had learners draw first without the text (retrieval) then revise with it, versus drawing with the text open; the closed-then-open format was not superior on tests at two and eight weeks. Mediation analysis suggested a gain via more retrieval offset by a loss via poorer mental-model quality. — [University of Münster record](https://cris-portal.uni-muenster.de/portal/en/publication/171862949)

### Inferences
- The safest claim is: producing the chart by hand is a stronger encoding act than looking at it, and skill at drawing is irrelevant. A study tool should therefore ask the player to sketch the chart, not just view it.
- Sketching from memory with immediate corrective feedback (compare with the true chart, fix errors) is supported indirectly by the general retrieval-practice literature and the Kim et al. mediation result (retrieval helps, but uncorrected poor drawings hurt). The Kim result warns that retrieval-drawing without good feedback can entrench a wrong picture.
- A chart that is designed to be drawable in a few strokes (a frame plus 2-4 boundary lines) is both within working-memory limits and suited to the drawing effect; a 169-cell coloured grid is not practically drawable, which removes this route to memory.
- The drawing effect evidence is for single words and for science text comprehension; no study tested redrawing a quantitative chart and later reasoning from it.

### Gaps
- Ainsworth, Prain & Tytler (2011, Science, "Drawing to learn in science") was not retrieved; it is a short argument piece rather than an experiment (from background knowledge).
- Exact recall proportions from Wammes et al. were not retrieved (I recall drawn items being recalled at roughly twice the rate of written items, unverified).
- No direct peer-reviewed test of "copy the diagram" versus "redraw the diagram from memory" on a delayed transfer test was found.
- The claim in the general retrieval-practice literature (Roediger & Karpicke) was not separately sourced here.

## 6. Prototype learning, perceptual learning and the role of verbal labels

### Takeaway
People readily abstract a prototype from varied examples and can then classify new instances against a small vocabulary of shapes; short classification drills with many varied examples build fast pattern recognition. Names help form and stabilise categories, but describing a hard-to-verbalise visual memory in words can modestly impair it, so labels should name the category, not replace the picture.

### Cited Findings
- Posner & Keele (1968, JEP 77, 353-363): after learning to classify distortions of 9-dot patterns, participants classified the never-seen prototype faster and more accurately than new distortions, and about as well as the patterns they had actually studied. — [Summary of the paper](https://support.pstnet.com/hc/en-us/articles/360050436814-STEP-On-the-Genesis-of-Abstract-Ideas-35277); [Cognitive toolkit summary](https://digfir-published.macmillanusa.com/cogtk2/concepts.html)
- Perceptual learning modules (Kellman, Massey & Son 2010, Topics in Cognitive Science 2, 285-305): brief classification trials across many varied instances, with feedback, produce "discovery" effects (learning which features and relations matter) and "fluency" effects (faster, more automatic extraction); applied in mathematics, aviation and medical image reading, with gains reported to persist at delayed tests. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6124488); [Kellman & Massey 2013](https://kellmanlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/357/2025/09/kellman_massey_2013.pdf)
- Labels and category learning: Lupyan, Rakison & McClelland (2007, Psychological Science): giving redundant nonsense names to two categories of novel "alien" shapes made the categories faster to learn, although the names added no information and all participants had equal feedback. — [Paper PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/psychology/infant-cognition-lab/publications/Lupyan,%20Rakison,%20&%20McClelland%20(2007).pdf)
- Contested: a replication attempt of Lupyan et al. Experiment 2 recorded in the FLoRA replication atlas failed to reproduce the faster learning with labels (single replication; details not checked). — [FORRT replication atlas](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.02028.x/)
- Verbal overshadowing: Schooler & Engstler-Schooler (1990) reported that describing a face reduced later line-up identification by 25%. The registered replication report (Alogna et al. 2014) found a robust but much smaller effect: about 4% when description immediately followed viewing, about 16% when description came after a 20-minute delay. — [Registered Replication Report](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/65957)
- Verbal framing changes what is remembered of a shape: a description implying symmetry made a near-symmetric curve be remembered as more symmetric (Tversky & Schiano 1989). — [Record](https://opact.sdu.edu.tr/vufind/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-a391t-a895675e27ba08337c115609670181625704ad80223f08e5c98b6fe68567ef13)
- Aphantasic participants compensate with verbal coding and retain spatial accuracy with fewer false memories (Bainbridge et al. 2021). — [Record](https://westminsterresearch.westminster.ac.uk/item/v2475/quantifying-aphantasia-through-drawing-those-without-visual-imagery-show-deficits-in-object-but-not-spatial-memory)

### Inferences
- A workable scheme is a small named vocabulary of prototype shapes (for a curve: e.g. a handful of archetypes; for a range: a handful of region shapes), learned by classifying many real instances with feedback (the perceptual learning module format). New charts are then stored as "prototype name plus deviation", which matches both prototype abstraction and the template idea in section 3.
- Labels pull memory toward the labelled prototype (Tversky & Schiano; schema effects). This is useful for holding gist but will erase small deviations, so any strategically important deviation needs its own explicit tag.
- Verbal overshadowing was shown for faces, a stimulus that resists verbal description; a deliberately designed schematic shape that is easy to describe should be less vulnerable, but this is an inference, not a tested result.
- The number of prototypes should be small enough to be discriminable at a glance; no source gives an optimal number, but working-memory limits and typical category-learning studies (2-4 categories) suggest starting with about four to six.

### Gaps
- Forgetting-rate claims for prototypes versus exemplars (Posner & Keele 1970) were not sourced.
- Brandimonte's work on verbal recoding of shapes (naming a shape at encoding impairs later image manipulation, and articulatory suppression removes the impairment) was not retrieved beyond the 1993 paper's existence; treat as background.
- No study found that tested prototype-plus-deviation coding for quantitative curves or matrices.
- Kellman et al. effect sizes were not retrieved.

## 7. Design implications reported in the literature for recallable diagrams

### Takeaway
The literature supports a consistent set of constraints: few parts (about 3-4), grouped into simple, symmetric or otherwise regular shapes, always placed on one fixed frame with named landmarks, with meaning carried by position and shape rather than colour, a verbal title stating the conclusion, and learning by classifying and redrawing. Most of these are extrapolations from basic memory research; almost none has been tested on charts that must be recalled and reasoned from.

### Cited Findings
- Number of elements: about 3-4 objects or chunks can be held (Luck & Vogel; Cowan), and imagery quality falls with each added item (Keogh & Pearson). — [Cowan 2001](https://memory.psych.missouri.edu/assets/doc/articles/2001/cowan-bbs-2001.pdf); [Keogh & Pearson 2017](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/cognitive-bias/illusion-of-depth/2017-keogh.pdf)
- Grouping and regularity: proximity and connectedness grouping improve retention (about 12% and 6% accuracy gains), learned regularities allow compression, and detectable symmetry improves recall. — [Review citing Woodman et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4068940); [Brady et al. 2009](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/a9b32c42-474e-4d98-83cb-9213f6bccce7); [Symmetry study](https://core.ac.uk/download/53131207.pdf)
- Fixed frame and configuration: items are remembered relative to the whole configuration (Jiang et al. 2000); expert chunks are tied to specific board locations and break under mirror reflection (Gobet & Simon 1996); graph lines are remembered relative to a conceptual reference line (Tversky & Schiano 1989). — [Review citing Jiang et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3806891); [Gobet & Simon 1996](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03200937); [Schiano & Tversky 1992](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03208249)
- Skeleton plus slots: templates let masters store several positions quickly (Gobet & Simon 1996); schema-congruent items are learned in one trial (Tse et al. 2007). — [Gobet 1998 review](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/815/1/FullText.pdf); [Tse et al. 2007 record](https://discovery.dundee.ac.uk/en/publications/schemas-and-memory-consolidation/)
- Colour: more colours raised recognition memorability of visualisations (Borkin 2013), but colour is the kind of object detail that is lost in recall from memory by people with weak imagery, while spatial layout is retained (Bainbridge 2021). — [Borkin 2013 page](https://vcg.seas.harvard.edu/publications/20130101-what-makes-a-visualization-memorable); [Bainbridge 2021 record](https://westminsterresearch.westminster.ac.uk/item/v2475/quantifying-aphantasia-through-drawing-those-without-visual-imagery-show-deficits-in-object-but-not-spatial-memory)
- Text and redundancy: titles and text are what people recall from a visualisation, and stating the message redundantly helps (Borkin 2016). — [Borkin 2016 page](https://vcg.seas.harvard.edu/publications/20150101-beyond-memorability-visualization-recognition-and-recall)
- Relevant pictorial elements help recall of theme; irrelevant ones hurt (follow-up to Bateman). — [Memorability of Enhanced Informational Graphics](https://journals.shareok.org/ijsw/article/view/54)
- Images are hard to reinterpret unless built from simple parts (Chambers & Reisberg 1985; Finke et al. 1989). — [Peterson et al. 1992](https://ocf.berkeley.edu/~jfkihlstrom/PDFs/1990s/1992/Peterson_etal1992.pdf); [Finke, Pinker & Farah](https://stevenpinker.com/publications/reinterpreting-visual-patterns-mental-imagery)
- Generation: drawing improves recall irrespective of drawing ability (Wammes et al. 2016). — [Waterloo repository](https://uwspace.uwaterloo.ca/items/a8b52734-18f3-4527-8be2-008a105c85f2)

### Inferences
Candidate design rules for a chart meant to be pictured without a device, each tagged with how direct the support is:
- Cap the picture at 3-4 regions or strokes on the frame (direct for simple arrays; extrapolated to charts).
- Make each region a simple connected shape with a straight or single-bend boundary; avoid scattered isolated cells (grouping evidence; extrapolated).
- Keep one canonical frame, orientation and axis order for every chart; never mirror or re-sort (strong support from chess mirror-image and context-change results).
- Give the frame a few named landmarks and express boundaries relative to them ("the line runs from landmark A to landmark B") (loci and expert-anchor evidence; extrapolated).
- Encode meaning in position and shape first; use at most 2-3 colours, redundantly (spatial vs object imagery evidence; the precise colour count is my judgement, not a reported result).
- Store each chart as a named prototype plus one or two deviations (prototype, template and schema evidence; untested for charts).
- Use a reference line and remember only the departure from it (graph-memory distortion evidence turned into a design choice; untested).
- Attach a short title that states the strategic conclusion (direct support from Borkin 2016 for recall of message).
- Build the answer to the at-the-table question into the encoded shape, because reading new facts off an image afterwards is unreliable (imagery reinterpretation evidence).
- Train by classify-with-feedback drills and by sketching from memory followed by correction (perceptual learning and drawing evidence; the from-memory variant is only indirectly supported).
- Exploit symmetry where it is real, but mark true asymmetries explicitly, since memory will otherwise regularise them away.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study that designs charts for later mental visualisation and tests whether people can reason from the remembered chart; this is the central untested link.
- No evidence-based optimum for colour count in recall (as opposed to recognition or perceptual discrimination) was found.
- No data on how long a learned schematic chart persists without rehearsal, or how many distinct charts within one frame can be kept apart before they interfere; interference between many similar charts on one frame is a plausible risk the chess template work only partly addresses.
- Hosts needed to verify primary full texts (vcg.seas.harvard.edu, pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) were not reachable from this session.
