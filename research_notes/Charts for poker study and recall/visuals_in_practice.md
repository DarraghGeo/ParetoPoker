# Visuals in practice: how strategy is pictured for study in poker and comparable expert domains

Method note for the report writer. Page fetching was blocked in this session for every host tried (tommyangelo.com, pokerbug.blogspot.com, help.gtowizard.com, splitsuit.com, pokercoaching.com, hal.archives-ouvertes.fr). Everything below therefore rests on web-search result summaries and snippets, not on full-text reading. Quotations are as the search tool returned them. Treat exact figures as "reported by the search summary of the source" and check the original before printing a number. Evidence class is tagged on each finding: [PEER] peer-reviewed research, [DOC] product documentation, [PRACT] practitioner or coach opinion, [COMM] forum/community, [SEC] secondary summary (e.g. Wikipedia, news).

## 1. Poker: which visual representations are used for study today, where did they come from, what are the criticisms, and what alternatives exist?

### Takeaway
The 13x13 grid's origin is undocumented in anything I could find; the only dated testimony is a poker author saying it "did not exist" when he wrote a 2007 book and that his chart was a long-hand precursor, which supports the owner's view that the grid is an indexing convenience (169 hand classes in a square) that arrived with range software rather than a format designed for learning. Solver tools layer more views on top (equity-vs-percentile curves, equity buckets, aggregated reports) and practitioner writing consistently says the value lies in understanding why, not in memorising the pictures; I found no peer-reviewed study of any poker strategy visual and no published, widely adopted alternative to the grid.

### Cited Findings

Origin and rationale of the 13x13 grid
- [PRACT] Tommy Angelo, on his Universal Starting-Hand Chart from *Elements of Poker*: "When I wrote Elements, the now-ubiquitous 13×13 grid of hold'em starting hands did not exist. The USHC was, in concept, a long-hand version of the 13×13 chart." This is the only dated statement on the grid's origin found; it does not say who first drew it. — [Tommy Angelo, USHC](https://www.tommyangelo.com/ushc)
- [SEC] The grid encodes the 169 strategically distinct starting hands: pocket pairs on the diagonal, suited hands above it, offsuit below. — [vip-grinders, ranges](https://www.vip-grinders.com/poker-strategy/ranges/)
- [SEC] PokerStove (freeware, RPS Consulting; last stable release 1.23 in 2008) was built to compute equity for ranges of hands rather than single hands, which is the use case a range-selection grid serves. The page does not say PokerStove originated the grid. — [Wikipedia, PokerStove](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/PokerStove)
- [SEC] Before the grid, starting-hand guidance was commonly expressed as ranked groups: an 8-group ranking attributed to Sklansky and Malmuth's *Hold'em Poker for Advanced Players*. — [U. Toronto page, Poker Hand Groupings](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~tfowler/PokerHandGroupings.html); [ThePokerBank, Sklansky groups](https://thepokerbank.com/strategy/basic/starting-hand-selection/sklansky-groups)
- [PRACT] A known weakness of the grid: each cell looks the same size but holds different numbers of combinations (pairs 6, suited 4, offsuit 12), so "a range can look narrow on the grid but hold many more combos than it appears"; the guide says you need the combo count alongside the grid. — [vip-grinders, ranges](https://www.vip-grinders.com/poker-strategy/ranges/)
- [COMM] A 2012 blog series on hand-chart construction devoted a whole post to chart format, showing the format was still treated as an open design choice then (content not readable; host blocked). — [Pokerbug blog, 2012](https://pokerbug.blogspot.com/2012/07/hand-chart-construction-part-2-format.html)

Equity distribution graph (equity vs percentile)
- [DOC] GTO Wizard's definition: the horizontal axis is the strength of your hand within your own range as a percentile; the vertical axis is that hand's equity against the opponent's range; each player is one line (e.g. BB blue, BTN green); dots mark combinations with at least 90% equity against the opponent's range (the nut-advantage cue). The article teaches by asking readers to match each graph to its flop. — [GTO Wizard blog, Interpreting Equity Distributions](https://blog.gtowizard.com/interpreting-equity-distributions/) (search snippet only)
- [DOC] The same source describes cases the graph is meant to reveal: one player with "a huge equity advantage that persists across all segments of hand strength", and a player with range advantage in the middle of the distribution who lacks the nut advantage. — [GTO Wizard blog, Interpreting Equity Distributions](https://blog.gtowizard.com/interpreting-equity-distributions/)
- [DOC] Axis conventions are not standard across tools: a PLO trainer plots the strongest hands on the left and air on the right, the reverse of the percentile-ascending layout. — [PLO Mastermind help, equity graphs](https://intercom.help/plo-mastermind/en/articles/12129411-4-card-plo-trainer-equity-graphs)
- [DOC] Other products have added the same graph recently (PeakGTO "Equity Graphs"; PocketSolver "equity chart"), so it is now a standard solver view. — [PokerCoaching, PeakGTO equity graphs](https://pokercoaching.com/blog/introducing-equity-graphs-in-peakgto/); [PocketSolver docs](https://www.pocketsolver.com/documentation/analyzingresults/equitychart)
- [SEC] Glossary framing: range advantage is one number per side; the equity distribution is the curve behind that number, and two ranges with the same average can call for different plans. — [pokerskill glossary, Equity Distribution](https://www.pokerskill.com/poker-glossary/equity-distribution/)
- [SEC] Matthew Janda's *Applications of No-Limit Hold'em* has a section "Hand Equity Distribution on Different Board Textures", so the concept predates the solver UIs. Whether the book introduced the graph is not established. — [book table of contents](https://www.obalkyknih.cz/file/toc/1007351/pdf)

Range morphology vocabulary (polarised, linear, merged, condensed, capped)
- [PRACT] Polarised = strong value plus bluffs with the middle removed; suits large bets, especially with nut advantage. — [DeucesCracked](https://www.deucescracked.com/blog/polarized-vs-merged-ranges-poker-betting-guide-2026)
- [PRACT] The vocabulary is inconsistent between writers: one treats "linear" and "condensed" as the same shape (strong down to medium); another defines linear as strict top-down value with no gaps, merged as a wider in-between shape, and condensed as clustered in the middle. — [DeucesCracked](https://www.deucescracked.com/blog/polarized-vs-merged-ranges-poker-betting-guide-2026); [pokerskill blog](https://www.pokerskill.com/blog/polarized-linear-merged-ranges/)
- [PRACT] The words are already used as a shape-to-action rule: range advantage plus nut advantage implies big polarised bets; range advantage alone implies small frequent merged bets; nut advantage without range advantage implies polarised play at lower frequency. Another site: range advantage tends to set bet frequency, nut advantage tends to set bet size. — [DeucesCracked](https://www.deucescracked.com/blog/polarized-vs-merged-ranges-poker-betting-guide-2026); [vip-grinders, range advantage](https://www.vip-grinders.com/poker-strategy/range-advantage/)

Equity buckets
- [SEC] An equity bucket is "a study and solver grouping that sorts hands in a range by similar equity and strategic role on a specific board". Example splits quoted from GTO Wizard-based analysis: in position roughly 57% strong / 37% good / 5% weak / 1% trash, against a big blind at roughly 7% / 12% / 0% / 81%. Exact bucket thresholds were not found. — [pokerskill glossary topic](https://www.pokerskill.com/poker-glossary/topics/ranges-gto-and-strategy/); [dataset excerpt of GTO Wizard-based text](https://huggingface.co/datasets/aniketarahane/llmsandpoker/viewer/default/train?p=55)

Aggregated flop reports and turn/runout reports
- [PRACT] An aggregated report "takes one preflop spot ... and solves it on a whole set of flops", from 25 up to all 1,755 strategically distinct flops. A turn report for one flop has one row per turn card listing check / bet-size / all-in frequencies, with an "all 49 turn cards" summary row. — [poker.academy, aggregated reports guide](https://poker.academy/blog/post/aggregated-reports-guide)
- [SEC] GTO Wizard's customisable aggregated flop reports launched as an Elite-tier feature (June 2025). — [PokerNews](https://www.pokernews.com/news/2025/06/gto-wizard-customizable-aggregated-reports-48758.htm)

Range-vs-board breakdowns (Flopzilla)
- [PRACT] Flopzilla takes a range and a board and shows how often the range connects, by made-hand class; described as "basically PokerStove on steroids" and explicitly not a solver substitute. — [ThePokerBank / SplitSuit, Using Flopzilla](https://www.thepokerbank.com/videos/splitsuit/using-flopzilla/); [vip-grinders, poker tools](https://www.vip-grinders.com/poker-tools/)

What coaches and writers say about learning from these visuals (all practitioner opinion)
- "The value is in understanding why it does what it does, not in memorizing the chart"; "a solver can show you the answer; it cannot teach you the question." — [DeucesCracked, solver study guide](https://www.deucescracked.com/blog/poker-solver-study-guide-efficient-workflow)
- Asking "what makes this hand a good bluff?" improves play faster "than building an enormous mental library of solver screenshots". — [London Loves Business](https://londonlovesbusiness.com/understanding-why-solvers-bluff-instead-of-memorising-them/) (low-authority outlet)
- The counter-view: charts are worth memorising, and the practical way to retain them is by border hands and category endpoints, not cell by cell. — [BetterPoker, memorising ranges](https://betterpoker.vercel.app/learn/preflop-mastery/preflop-in-practice/memorizing-ranges); [vgorti blog](https://vgorti.pages.dev/posts/gto_explained_2)
- Practitioners already publish solver output compressed into verbal heuristics for a spot. — [Judah Chambers, Simple GTO-based heuristics](https://judahchambers.substack.com/p/simple-gto-based-heuristics-btn-vs)

Alternative visualisations and academic work
- [PRACT] For PLO, where a 13x13 grid cannot hold four-card hands, one trainer uses a 13x13 matrix split by good and bad suits: a modification of the grid, not a replacement. — [PLO Mastermind help, preflop matrix](https://intercom.help/plo-mastermind/en/articles/11892120-4-card-plo-preflop-matrix)
- [PEER, poster] The only visualisation-venue poker work found is a EuroVis 2025 poster, "Visual Analysis of Poker Hands for Individual Players" (Dotzler, Agarwal, Beck): line chart of cumulative winnings, scatterplot, bar chart and an event-sequence view for hand review. It is about reviewing play, not representing strategy. — [Eurographics Digital Library](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.2312/evp20251145)
- [Preprint] A 2023 arXiv paper builds an equilibrium-invariant embedding of 2x2 normal-form games and uses it to visualise the strategy space of Kuhn poker and other small extensive-form games. Research-facing, not a study aid. — [arXiv 2304.09978](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.09978)
- [PEER] CHI 2021, "What Players Want: Information Needs of Players on Post-Game Visualizations", studies what players want from visuals for reviewing and improving performance (games generally, not poker). — [OpenAlex record](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1145/3411764.3445174)

### Inferences
- The grid is a lookup index. Its axes are card ranks, not anything strategic; hand strength runs roughly along a diagonal gradient that the player must learn separately, and cell area misstates frequency. Nothing found suggests it was designed or tested for retention.
- The equity-vs-percentile curve is the closest existing thing to the owner's goal, because its shape already has a named vocabulary (polarised, linear, condensed, capped) and practitioners already attach action rules to the shape. Its weaknesses for recall: two overlaid curves must be compared by eye, the conventions differ by tool (axis direction), the names are used inconsistently, and the curve shows equity only, not EV, playability or blockers.
- The practitioner rule "range advantage sets frequency, nut advantage sets size" is in effect a two-cue decision rule. That is the natural bridge to fast-and-frugal trees (section 5) and to a glyph with two salient features (section 4).
- The way coaches recommend memorising grids (borders and endpoints) says players already compress the grid to a boundary line. A study chart could show the boundary directly.

### Gaps
- Who first drew the 13x13 grid and in which product or book: not found. Candidate early carriers (PokerStove, Poker Equilab, Flopzilla, early 2000s forum posts) could not be dated from sources.
- I could not read documentation for PioSOLVER, GTO+, Simple Postflop, DTO, Odin or Lucid; nothing specific to those interfaces is reported here. Strategy bars, EV and equity-realisation heatmaps, blockers views, hand-class tables and "manhattan" 3D plots are known features of these tools but I found no citable description in this session, so they are not characterised above.
- GTO Wizard's exact equity-bucket thresholds: not found (help.gtowizard.com blocked; blog reachable only as snippets).
- No published scatter of "equity advantage vs nut advantage" across flops was found, though aggregated reports make one possible.
- No peer-reviewed or controlled comparison of learning outcomes from any poker visual, and none comparing memorising charts with learning heuristics.
- No academic HCI/visualisation paper on representing poker ranges or mixed strategies for learners was found. Absence in one session of searching is not proof of absence.

## 2. Chess, Go, bridge, backgammon: which visual study aids are built for recall, and what evidence or respected opinion says they help?

### Takeaway
The solid evidence is about how experts hold positions in memory (as familiar chunks and templates with a fixed core and variable slots), not about which printed study aid works best; backgammon is the clearest case of a community turning engine output into a small set of memorised reference numbers and mental-arithmetic rules. I found no controlled study showing that opening trees, pawn-structure diagrams or Go influence maps improve learning.

### Cited Findings
- [PEER] Chase and Simon (1973): masters reconstruct real game positions far better than novices after brief exposure, with no (or little) advantage on random positions; they proposed experts hold on the order of 50,000 chunks. — [Chase & Simon 1973 (copy)](https://andymatuschak.org/prompts/Chase1973.pdf); [notes on the paper](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Chase_and_Simon_-_Perception_in_chess)
- [PEER] Later re-analysis found a small but reliable skill effect even on random positions, explained by stronger players recognising familiar small patterns that occur by chance. — [Gobet & Simon (repository copy)](https://core.ac.uk/download/334663.pdf)
- [PEER] Template theory (Gobet and Simon): frequently used chunks become templates, schema-like structures with "a core of constant information and slots for variable information"; this explains fast recall at short presentation times. — [Gobet, PMC article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3217924); [repository copy](https://core.ac.uk/download/333524.pdf)
- [PEER] A CHREST chunk-based model categorised 500 positions from the French and Sicilian by opening about as well as a support-vector machine: position type is recoverable from learned chunks. This models expert memory; it does not test a study aid. — [LSE Research Online](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/102853)
- [COMM] Go: forum users note that influence/territory maps "can mislead beginners", that engines do not use such functions in play, but that they "could be an aid to humans to help understand go positions"; also that AlphaGo's publicised heat map showed next-move probability, not influence. Opinion only. — [Life in 19x19 forum](https://lifein19x19.com/viewtopic.php?p=200991); [OGS forum](https://forums.online-go.com/t/influence-in-go-visualised/3224)
- [PRACT] Backgammon race decisions are reduced to a pip count plus a short threshold rule. Keith count: adjust the raw pip count for checker distribution, then "double if your count exceeds your opponent's by no more than 4; redouble if ... no more than 3; take if doubler's count exceeds your count by at least 2". — [bkgm.com, Keith's pip counting article](https://bkgm.com/articles/Keith/KangarooCount/); [bkgm.com articles index](https://bkgm.com/articles/)
- [PRACT] Kleinman's 1980 method gives race winning chances from two numbers, the pip difference D and the pip sum S (published variants: K = (D+4)^2/(S-4) and the simpler D^2/S; sources disagree on which). — [Zeilberger et al., long race paper](https://sites.math.rutgers.edu/~zeilberg/expmath/LongRaceAB.pdf); [rec.games.backgammon thread](https://groups.google.com/g/rec.games.backgammon/c/73yUbzlmzRo/m/On3x1wt6Ds4J)
- [PRACT] The backgammon community has many competing mental pip-counting schemes built around visual clusters of checkers (Kissane's cluster counting, Kangaroo count and others), i.e. the picture is deliberately chunked into shapes with known values. — [bkgm.com articles](https://bkgm.com/articles/)

### Inferences
- Template theory is the best scientific description of what a "chart a player can picture" should be: a small fixed core (the canonical shape) with a few slots that take the values of the current situation. A poker chart that varies in two or three slots is consistent with how chess experts are thought to store positions.
- Backgammon shows the full pipeline the owner wants: bot-derived truth, compressed to one derived number (adjusted pip count), compared against memorised cut-offs (4 / 3 / 2). The picture is not a chart of the solution; it is a procedure for computing a number from the board plus a threshold.
- Where a domain shows learners an "information dump" overlay (Go influence maps), experienced players are sceptical; the aids that last are the ones that name a pattern and attach an action.

### Gaps
- Robertie's reference-position method and "501 Essential Backgammon Problems": no source retrieved. The claim that backgammon study is organised around memorised reference positions is widely held but uncited here.
- Bridge hand-evaluation aids (4-3-2-1 point count, losing trick count, Law of Total Tricks) were not searched for lack of budget; nothing is cited.
- No controlled evidence found on chess opening trees, pawn-structure diagrams, piece-activity heatmaps or Go territory maps as learning aids.

## 3. Other domains where a picture must be recalled and reasoned from under time pressure

### Takeaway
The strongest direct evidence is the mental abacus: people really do compute on an imagined picture, but the picture is stored as about three or four column-sized units, and the skill depends on the learner's existing spatial working memory. Medicine and aviation evidence points the other way for rare, high-stakes events (an external checklist beats memory), while the cognitive-science account of diagrams says a picture helps only when it makes the needed inference a matter of perception rather than computation.

### Cited Findings
- [PEER] Frank and Barner (2012), *J Exp Psychol: General* 141(1):134-149: mental abacus "is represented in visual working memory by splitting the abacus into a series of columns, each of which is independently stored as a unit". Users appear unable to hold more than about three columns at once; adding beads within the same columns had no effect on performance. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21767040/); [phys.org summary](https://phys.org/news/2011-08-stanford-explores-language.html)
- [PEER] Barner et al. (2016), *Child Development* 87(4): three-year study, 204 children aged 5 to 7 at start; mental-abacus students outperformed controls on arithmetic; training did not change basic cognitive abilities; baseline spatial working memory mediated how well children learned the technique; users limited to three or four columns. — [Harvard DASH copy](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/handle/1/41364831/Barneretal.inpress.pdf?sequence=1)
- [PEER] A one-year classroom-randomised follow-up found first-graders struggled to reach abacus expertise while second-graders did better; authors advise caution on mental abacus as a short-term intervention. — [PsychArchives / J. Numerical Cognition](https://www.psycharchives.org/handle/20.500.12034/1271)
- [PEER] Larkin and Simon (1987), "Why a Diagram is (Sometimes) Worth Ten Thousand Words": diagrams group related information by location, cutting search, and "display information that is only implicit in sentential representations" so that inferences become perceptual; the benefit depends on the reader having the operations to recognise the relevant pattern. — [paper copy, UCSD](https://mechanism.ucsd.edu/bill/teaching/F12/cs200/Readings/larkin.whyadiagramissometimesworth.1987.pdf); [SERC summary](https://serc.carleton.edu/resources/961.html)
- [PEER] Arriaga et al. (2013, NEJM): 17 operating-room teams, 106 simulated crises, each team managing half with checklists and half from memory; failure to perform critical steps fell from 23% to 6% with the checklist. Simulation only. — [2 Minute Medicine summary](https://www.2minutemedicine.com/surgical-crisis-checklists-improve-performance-during-operating-room-crisis-simulations/); [Clinician.com summary](https://www.clinician.com/articles/64033-crisis-checklists-improve-management-of-rarely-occurring-events)
- [PEER] A 2022 randomised simulation trial of medical emergencies (80 teams, 240 scenarios) found a smaller benefit from cognitive aids: 9% absolute, 15% relative risk reduction. — [Scand J Trauma Resusc Emerg Med](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1186/s13049-022-01028-y)
- [PEER] Risk pictures in medicine: icon arrays reduce denominator neglect, especially in low-numeracy readers (Garcia-Retamero and Galesic; Galesic, Garcia-Retamero and Gigerenzer 2009). Icon type changes both risk perception and risk recall (six icon types tested; person-shaped "restroom" icons did best). Scattering the icons randomly rather than grouping them changes perceived probability. — [Garcia-Retamero & Galesic, MDM 2010](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0272989X10369000); [Zikmund-Fisher et al. 2014, MDM](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/medema/v34y2014i4p443-453.html); [JDM, icon arrays in repeated risky choice](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/45A8ACE495DC54F9B082E9D385CBDE80/S1930297500009153a.pdf/div-class-title-effects-of-icon-arrays-to-communicate-risk-in-a-repeated-risky-decision-making-task-div.pdf)
- [PRACT/DOC] Aviation energy management: the FAA Airplane Flying Handbook frames flying as managing altitude and airspeed as one energy state; errors are either "total energy errors" (both deviate the same way, fixed with power) or "energy distribution errors" (they deviate oppositely, fixed with pitch). Energy tables and circular diagrams are offered as conceptual tools so pilots think about what to do near the edges. — [AVweb, Energy Errors](https://avweb.com/flight-safety/technique/energy-errors/)
- [PEER, conceptual] Merkt (2013) argues energy management is not adequately taught and that an energy-centred, top-down model would improve understanding; it is an argument, not an outcome study. — [J. Aviation Technology and Engineering](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/jate/vol3/iss1/6)
- [PEER] Sport: experts recall structured game patterns far better than novices, and the advantage shrinks for random arrangements (Allard, Graham and Paarsalu 1980 for basketball; Garland and Barry 1991 using schematic sport diagrams, where experts' recall after an 8-second look was durable against interference). — [Encyclopedia of Sport and Exercise Psychology, pattern recognition and recall](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/embed/encyclopedia-of-sport-and-exercise-psychology/chpt/pattern-recognition-recall); [Garland & Barry 1991](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF02686899)

### Inferences
- The mental abacus sets a concrete design budget: a picture meant to be imagined and operated on should have about three, at most four, independently varying parts, each simple enough to be one unit. Detail inside a part is cheap; additional parts are expensive. A 169-cell grid or a free-form curve fails this budget; a shape with three slots meets it.
- The mental abacus works because the physical device and the image are the same object and the operations are practised on the device first. A poker glyph should be the same picture in the study tool and in the head, and the trainer should drill the operations (compare two shapes, read off the action), not just display the shape.
- Larkin and Simon's condition matters: the chart must make the strategic question a perceptual judgement (which line is higher at the top, where is the gap) and the learner must be taught the reading operations. The energy-management example is the same idea: two variables collapsed into one state plus two error types, each with one fix.
- The checklist evidence is a caution: for rare, complex situations memory loses to an aid. A recalled chart is suited to frequent, classifiable spots, not to rare ones.
- Baseline spatial working memory mediated abacus learning, so an imagery-based chart will suit some players better than others.

### Gaps
- Nomograms versus risk charts for clinician recall: nothing found.
- Navigation, music notation and chord diagrams, military playbook conventions, football playbook diagrams specifically, and canonical layouts such as the periodic table: not searched or nothing found; no claims made.
- Zhang and Norman's "representational effect" (same abstract structure, different external representation, different difficulty) is relevant but I retrieved only a bibliography entry, not findings.
- V-n diagrams and memory items in aviation: nothing found.

## 4. Glyph and schematic design: what does research say about compact shapes that carry meaning?

### Takeaway
Glyph research is cautionary: Chernoff faces and star glyphs have performed poorly in controlled tests, simple abstract glyphs beat face-like ones, and established guidance asks for few dimensions, redundant encoding and visually separable parts. Named shape vocabularies can be learned and recognised regardless of whether they carry valid information, as candlestick patterns show.

### Cited Findings
- [PEER] Fuchs, Isenberg, Bezerianos and Keim (2017), *IEEE TVCG* 23(7):1863-1879, systematically reviewed 64 user-study papers on data glyphs and derived design implications. Face-like glyphs were the most common experimental condition, with no clear consensus on their usefulness. — [LISN publication page](https://www.lisn.upsaclay.fr/publications/a-systematic-review-of-experimental-studies-on-data-glyphs/); [Tableau Research altCHI paper](https://www.tableau.com/es-es/research/publications/ross-chernoff-glyphs-or-how-do-we-kill-bad-ideas-visualization)
- [PEER] Lee, Butavicius and Reilly (2003) compared Chernoff faces and star glyphs with spatial displays for binary data: both glyph types led to "slow, inaccurate answers being given with low confidence". — [CRPIT abstract](https://crpit.scem.westernsydney.edu.au/abstracts/CRPITV24Lee.html)
- [PEER, alt.chi] "Ross-Chernoff Glyphs Or: How Do We Kill Bad Ideas in Visualization?" reports that traditional glyphs made of abstract bars and lines outperformed face-like glyphs across a variety of tasks. — [Tableau Research](https://www.tableau.com/es-es/research/publications/ross-chernoff-glyphs-or-how-do-we-kill-bad-ideas-visualization)
- [PEER] Borgo et al.'s glyph state-of-the-art report gives design guidelines including normalising each dimension's input, using redundant mappings, and keeping glyph components visually orthogonal; a later survey notes these cannot be followed for a high number of dimensions. — [Konstanz state-of-the-art survey citing Borgo et al.](https://scibib.dbvis.de/uploadedFiles/StateOfTheArt.pdf)
- [PEER] Fuchs's star-glyph experiments: glyphs without contours made it easier to detect data similarity. (From the author's project page, separate from the 2017 review.) — [Univ. Konstanz, J. Fuchs](https://www.vis.uni-konstanz.de/en/members/fuchs)
- [PEER] Borkin et al. (2013), 2,070 visualisations: those containing pictograms or human-recognisable objects were more memorable on average; colour and visual complexity also helped. The 2015 follow-up found pictograms did not interfere when used appropriately and that titles and text were central to what people recalled. — [Harvard VCG, 2013 paper](https://vcg.seas.harvard.edu/publications/20130101-what-makes-a-visualization-memorable); [Harvard VCG, 2015 paper](https://vcg.seas.harvard.edu/publications/20150101-beyond-memorability-visualization-recognition-and-recall)
- [PRACT] Stephen Few's critique: the Borkin study measured what is noticed in a one-second glance and recognised later, not whether content is remembered; "a visualization is not memorable unless its content is memorable". — [Perceptual Edge](https://www.perceptualedge.com/blog/?p=1770)
- [SEC/PEER] Sparklines (Tufte, *Beautiful Evidence*, 2006): "data-intense, design-simple, word-sized" graphics. Evaluation is thin and mixed: some studies in medical and accounting tables found faster pattern comparison than tables alone; one trend-question study found no difference in time or error; Goffin et al. found positive effects of word-scale graphics on information gain. — [Wikipedia, Sparkline](https://Www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparkline); [EuroVis 2018 short paper](https://diglib.eg.org/bitstream/handle/10.2312/eurp20181119/017-019.pdf); [NJIT thesis](https://digitalcommons.njit.edu/theses/402)
- [PEER] Candlestick patterns, a named shape vocabulary in wide use: Marshall, Young and Rose found candlestick strategies not generally profitable on large US stocks (1992-2002); a Thai SET50 study (2006-2016) found most patterns' returns not different from zero. — [Marshall, Young & Rose 2007, RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ris/jofitr/0834.html); [Tharavanij et al. 2017, SAGE Open](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/sagope/v7y2017i4p2158244017736799.html)

### Inferences
- A poker glyph should not map many variables onto many features of an iconic object. The evidence favours a plain abstract mark with few parts, where each part is one quantity on a common scale and the important contrast is encoded twice (e.g. position and fill).
- The existing equity curve is closer to a "profile glyph" than to a star or face, which is the family that the cited critiques favour (bars and lines).
- Memorability research supports giving each canonical shape a name and a recognisable silhouette, with the name doing much of the recall work (titles were what people remembered most). Poker already has the names; they need one fixed drawing each and one agreed definition.
- Candlesticks show the risk: a vocabulary of named shapes spreads and persists on recognisability alone. Each named poker shape should be validated against solver output (does the shape actually predict the action?) before it is taught.

### Gaps
- I could not read the Fuchs et al. review itself, so its specific conclusions on star versus profile glyphs and on the number of dimensions are not reported.
- No study found on how recognisable or learnable candlestick patterns are as shapes; the evidence found concerns only their predictive value.
- Data comics (Bach, Riche and colleagues): no findings retrieved.
- Named distribution shapes (skewed, bimodal, uniform) as a taught vocabulary: no research retrieved on how reliably people classify them.
- No guidance found that is specific to silhouette design for recall from memory, as opposed to reading on a page.

## 5. Decision heuristics as pictures: are fast-and-frugal trees learnable, memorable and accurate?

### Takeaway
Fast-and-frugal trees (a short ordered list of yes/no cues, each with an exit) have matched logistic regression in benchmark simulations and beaten physicians' judgement in the classic coronary-care case, and they do best when learning data is scarce. I found no direct experimental evidence on how well people memorise or recall them; the "easy to remember" claim rests on their size.

### Cited Findings
- [PEER] Green and Mehr (1997), *J Family Practice* 45(3):219-226: a three-question tree for coronary care admission (ST-segment change? chest pain the chief complaint? any one of five other factors?). Secondary reports say it had higher sensitivity and a lower false-positive rate than both the Heart Disease Predictive Instrument and physicians' own judgement; physicians had been sending about 90% of chest-pain patients to the unit. — [Marewski & Gigerenzer, Heuristic decision making in medicine, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3341653); [original paper PDF](https://cdn.mdedge.com/files/s3fs-public/jfp-archived-issues/1997-volume_44-45/JFP_1997-09_v45_i3_making-decisions-about-admitting-patient.pdf)
- [PEER] Martignon, Katsikopoulos and Woike (2008): across 30 real-world data sets, fast-and-frugal tree performance was comparable with logistic regression. — [reported in Jenny et al. 2013, JARMAC](https://dwulff.github.io/_Goodchoices/Literature/JennyEtAl2013SimpleRulesForDetectingDepression.pdf)
- [PEER] Luan, Schooler and Gigerenzer (2011), *Psychological Review* 118(2):316-338: the tree's exit structure corresponds to the decision criterion in signal detection theory (earlier "signal" exits make the tree more liberal); overall sensitivity depends on the cues' own sensitivities and inter-correlations rather than on exit structure. — [paper, MPG repository](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_2099024_9/component/file_2099023/SLU_Signal_2011.pdf)
- [SEC] Trees perform best relative to other models when the learning sample is small (e.g. fewer than 80 cases). — [Wikipedia, Fast-and-frugal trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast-and-frugal_trees)
- [PEER] Not always a win: a 2025 asset-pricing study found logistic regression outperformed a fast-and-frugal tree in predicting S&P 500 returns. — [Mind & Society 2025 (Dundee repository)](https://discovery.dundee.ac.uk/ws/files/158134462/s11299-025-00351-y.pdf)
- [SEC] Adoption is not automatic: in the coronary-care case "many doctors didn't like using it". — [Research Live](https://www.research-live.com/article/features/be360-nudging-our-white-coats/id/5033792)
- [PEER] Tooling exists to fit such trees to data: the FFTrees R package (Phillips, Neth, Woike and Gaissmaier, 2017, *Judgment and Decision Making*). — [paper copy, Univ. Basel](https://edoc-vmtest.ub.unibas.ch/56351/3/jdm17217.pdf)
- [PRACT] Backgammon's Keith count thresholds (section 2) and poker's "range advantage sets frequency, nut advantage sets size" (section 1) are practitioner examples of the same form: one or two cues, fixed cut-offs. — [bkgm.com](https://bkgm.com/articles/Keith/KangarooCount/); [vip-grinders](https://www.vip-grinders.com/poker-strategy/range-advantage/)

### Inferences
- Solver output is abundant, noise-free data, which is the opposite of the small-sample setting where trees beat complex models. The case for a tree in poker is therefore human capacity (three cues fit in the head), not statistical accuracy; the tree will lose some EV against the full solution and the size of that loss is measurable from aggregated reports. FFTrees-style fitting could be run on aggregated flop reports to find the cues and cut-offs.
- The signal-detection result gives a design lever: the order of exits sets the bias. A poker tree can be made to err toward the cheaper mistake (e.g. checking too often rather than over-betting) by choosing which cue exits first.
- A tree and a glyph can be the same object: if the glyph has three parts and the tree has three cues, each cue is "look at this part of the shape", which satisfies both the three-column imagery budget (section 3) and the tree form.

### Gaps
- No experiment found that measures recall or retention of fast-and-frugal trees against scoring systems or charts; the memorability claim is unsupported by direct evidence in what I found.
- No published fast-and-frugal tree for any poker decision was found.
- Exact sensitivity and false-positive figures for Green and Mehr come from secondary sources and were not verified against the original paper.
