# Learning with visual representations: evidence for designing study charts

Reliability note for the report writer. In this session only web search worked; every direct page fetch (PMC, Springer, arXiv, university hosts) was blocked by the network allowlist. So every finding below was checked against search-result abstracts and summaries, not full texts. Each item is tagged:

- **[abstract]** = figure or claim appears in the paper's own abstract or publisher/repository listing as returned by search.
- **[secondary]** = seen only in a summary written by someone other than the authors.
- Anything I only recall from training and could not confirm is in the Gaps lists, never in Cited Findings.

## 1. Multiple external representations (Ainsworth DeFT, Rau, Kozma & Russell)

### Takeaway
Several linked views help only when each has a distinct job and the learner is taught how they map onto each other; the mapping is the hard, costly part and novices do not do it spontaneously. Rau's work says two different skills are needed (explaining the mapping, and seeing it fast), each needs its own kind of practice, and the best-supported order is explanation first, speeded practice second.

### Cited Findings
- DeFT (Ainsworth 2006, Learning and Instruction 16(3), 183-198) names three functions of multiple representations: complement (different information or different processes), constrain (one representation, usually the familiar one, guides the reading of another), construct (deeper understanding from relating them). [abstract/secondary] — [Nottingham repository listing](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/output/23577631/deft-a-conceptual-framework-for-considering-learning-with-multiple-representations)
- DeFT's design parameters: number of representations, how information is distributed across them, the form of each, their sequence, and support for translation between them. The cognitive demand unique to multiple representations is translating between two of them, and "there is much evidence that this is complicated". [secondary] — [Ainsworth workshop paper, IWM Tübingen](https://www.iwm-tuebingen.de/workshops/visualization/ainsworth.pdf)
- Rau separates two connection-making skills: sense-making (verbally explaining how representations map onto each other) and perceptual fluency (linking them quickly from visual features without deliberate reasoning). They are learned through different processes and need different support. [secondary, from Rau-lab papers] — [Rau, Rummel & Aleven, ICLS 2014](https://website.education.wisc.edu/rau-lab/pubs/RauRummelAleven_ICLS2014_camera-ready_final_fixed.pdf)
- In a fractions tutoring study, neither sense-making support nor fluency-building support helped alone; the combination did (an interaction effect). [secondary] — [Rau, Rummel & Aleven, ICLS 2014](https://website.education.wisc.edu/rau-lab/pubs/RauRummelAleven_ICLS2014_camera-ready_final_fixed.pdf)
- Order: one Rau study reports support for the sense-making-first hypothesis and not for the fluency-first hypothesis. [secondary] — [Rau-lab paper via NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10421205)
- Boundary condition: in a chemistry study the advantage of sense-making and fluency activities over control appeared only for students with high prior chemistry knowledge. [secondary] — [Rau-lab paper via NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10421205)
- Boundary condition: in an astronomy video-game study, sense-making supports did not improve content learning while perceptual-fluency supports did; the pattern does not generalise cleanly across domains. [secondary] — [Rau-lab paper via NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10421205)
- Kozma & Russell (1997, J. Research in Science Teaching 34, 949-968): chemists and undergraduates sorted videos, graphs, animations and equations. Novices formed smaller groups, mostly of the same medium, justified by surface features; experts formed larger cross-media groups justified conceptually, and were better at converting a given representation into another, especially into words. Origin of the term "representational competence". [abstract/secondary] — [paper summary](https://researchhub.com/paper/7521790/multimedia-and-understanding-expert-and-novice-responses-to-different-representations-of-chemical-phenomena); [Kozma 2001 AERA paper](https://ctlweb.sri.com/chemsense/about/papers/KozmaAffordancesAERA2001.pdf)

### Inferences
- Grid, equity curve, range strips and a strength-by-volatility map are four representations of one strategy. By DeFT each needs a stated job (e.g. grid = look up a hand; strips = compare two ranges; map = see which hands change), otherwise the added views are pure translation cost.
- Use the familiar view to constrain the new one: tapping a hand in the 13x13 grid should light the same hand in the strip or map, and the reverse. This is direct translation support, the DeFT parameter most often missing.
- Novices will treat each chart as a separate thing tied to its surface look (Kozma & Russell). "Same hand, three places" highlighting and a one-line plain-English mapping are the likely remedy.
- Rau's sequence maps onto a product flow: first a slow explained walkthrough ("this strip is the same range as this grid, sorted by strength"), later quick-fire "which grid matches this strip?" drills. Skipping straight to drills is the less supported order.

### Gaps
- Rau (2017), "Conditions for the effectiveness of multiple visual representations in enhancing STEM learning", Educational Psychology Review: I could not open it; only its supporting experiments were seen in summaries. No effect sizes for Rau's studies were retrieved.
- Which specific Rau paper each boundary condition comes from could not be pinned down; the report should cite them as "Rau and colleagues" rather than by year.
- No study found on multiple representations in games of strategy or poker.

## 2. Perceptual learning with representations (Kellman & Massey)

### Takeaway
Short, rapid classification drills, where the learner matches or sorts many varied examples across representations with feedback and never solves a full problem, improve later problem solving and the gains last. Reported effects are positive but range from small to moderate in a larger classroom trial.

### Cited Findings
- Kellman, Massey & Son (2010), "Perceptual Learning Modules in Mathematics", Topics in Cognitive Science 2(2), 285-305: three modules tested in middle and high school. Practice in matching function information across representations (graphs, equations, word problems) helped students generate correct graphs and equations from word problems, a task not practised in the module. [abstract] — [PMC6124488](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6124488)
- The Algebraic Transformations module had 8th-9th graders map equations to legal transformations of them, without practising equation solving; gains were reported to persist over substantial delays. Later papers describe the effects as large. [secondary, qualitative] — [PMC6092017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6092017)
- A later randomised classroom trial of fraction and measurement modules reported effect sizes of 0.14 to 0.48; area and linear measurement modules showed larger and more consistent effects than fraction modules. [abstract of a 2016 conference poster, not peer-reviewed in that form] — [Massey et al. IES poster](https://kellmanlab.psych.ucla.edu/files/massey_ies_pl_mtg_poster_2016.pdf)

### Inferences
- The proposed "small vocabulary of named shapes" is a perceptual-classification target. The evidence supports training it by many quick varied trials ("which shape is this equity curve?", "which board gives this strip?") with immediate feedback, not by one explanatory page.
- Transfer in these studies came from cross-representation mapping trials, so drills should cross views (curve to strip, strip to grid), not only name a shape within one view.
- Per section 1, these drills belong after the meaning has been explained.

### Gaps
- Exact pre/post numbers, sample sizes, delay length and effect sizes for the 2010 paper were not retrievable; treat "large and durable" as the authors' own characterisation.
- Most evidence comes from one research group; I found no independent meta-analysis of perceptual learning modules.

## 3. Concreteness fading; iconic versus abstract encodings

### Takeaway
Starting with a concrete, recognisable form and fading to an idealised one is the best-supported compromise, but the evidence base is small and its generality is disputed. The famous claim that abstract examples transfer better (Kaminski et al. 2008) is contested, with a failed critical replication.

### Cited Findings
- Fyfe, McNeil, Son & Goldstone (2014), Educational Psychology Review 26(1), 9-25, is a systematic review (not a meta-analysis) of concreteness fading: a three-step progression from concrete/enactive, through iconic/pictorial, to symbolic/abstract, after Bruner. [abstract/secondary] — [Indiana University listing](https://pc.cogs.indiana.edu/?p=885)
- The direct transfer evidence is McNeil & Fyfe (2012), "Concreteness fading promotes transfer of mathematical knowledge", Learning and Instruction. [title only verified] — [PDF listing](https://pcl.sitehost.iu.edu/rgoldsto/courses/cogscilearning/McNeilFyfe12.pdf)
- A 2020 Educational Psychology Review article argues the broad applicability of the fading sequence is in doubt. [secondary] — [Springer listing](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-020-09581-7)
- Kaminski, Sloutsky & Heckler (2008), Science 320, 454, "The advantage of abstract examples in learning math", claimed better transfer from one generic example than from concrete ones. — [citation record](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/kaminski-sloutsky-heckler-theadvantageofabstractexamplesinlearningmath-2008)
- De Bock et al. (2011) argued the transfer task in that study was itself abstract, so it tested abstract-to-abstract transfer; adding a concrete transfer task, they replicated the original result and also found concrete learners transferred better to the concrete task. [secondary] — [CogSci proceedings paper summarising the debate](https://journalpub.escholarship.org/cognitivesciencesociety/article/28613/galley/18484/download)
- Trninic, Kapur & Sinha (2020, CogSci) ran a critical replication and report results that "do not support the hypothesized advantage of abstract examples; on the contrary, they favor the concrete example". [abstract; conference paper] — [Trninic et al. 2020](https://edrl.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/Trninic-2020-CogSci.pdf)

### Inferences
- Contested: do not tell the owner that "abstract is better for transfer". The safer reading is that transfer follows the match between what was studied and what is tested, and that fading gives both grounding and generality.
- For the poker charts: introduce a shape on real, named hands on a concrete board (cards visible on the strip), then fade to the bare strip or curve outline with the name only. The named-shape vocabulary is the idealised end state, not the starting point.

### Gaps
- Goldstone & Son (2005), the original fading experiment, was not retrieved; no effect sizes for concreteness fading were found.
- No pooled effect size exists that I could find; the 2014 review is narrative.
- I found nothing specific on iconic versus abstract mark encodings (e.g. card pictures versus dots) for novice chart readers.

## 4. Interactive and dynamic visualisations; prediction before reveal

### Takeaway
Motion and interactivity give small average gains that depend heavily on whether the motion itself is the thing to be learned and on how much guidance surrounds it; unguided exploration is the weak case. Asking the learner to predict or draw the answer before revealing the chart is a cheap interaction with direct evidence for better recall.

### Cited Findings
- Höffler & Leutner (2007): 26 studies, 76 pairwise comparisons, animation over static pictures d = 0.37 (95% CI 0.25-0.49). Larger when the animation is representational rather than decorative, when it is realistic video, and for procedural-motor knowledge. [abstract] — [Leibniz IPN listing](https://www.leibniz-ipn.de/en/research/publications/instructional-animation-versus-static-pictures-a-meta-analysis)
- Berney & Bétrancourt (2016): 140 pairwise comparisons, g = 0.226 (95% CI 0.12-0.33), a small effect. [abstract] — [University of Geneva archive](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:92234)
- A later re-analysis of the Berney & Bétrancourt data (Ploetzner and colleagues) found larger animation effects when the specifics of the displayed changes were what had to be learned. [secondary] — [Instructional Science article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-021-09541-w)
- Simulations: adding some form of learner guidance to a simulation had a modest effect on learning outcomes (d = 0.49) and inquiry skills (d = 0.41), from D'Angelo et al. (2014) as reported inside Lazonder & Harmsen. [secondary] — [Lazonder & Harmsen 2016 text](https://typeset.io/pdf/meta-analysis-of-inquiry-based-learning-effects-of-guidance-3qc3oxe7im.pdf)
- Lazonder & Harmsen (2016), Review of Educational Research 86(3), 681-718: guidance in inquiry learning had significant positive effects on learning activities, performance success and learning outcomes. [secondary; pooled values not retrieved] — [TUM Clearinghouse summary](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/CHU_KR-5_Lazonder-Harmsen_2016_Forschendes-Lernen.pdf)
- Kim, Reinecke & Hullman (CHI 2017, Best Paper), "Explaining the Gap": participants who predicted the data graphically and/or self-explained, then saw feedback on the gap between their prediction and the real data, outperformed a control group on recall and comprehension. Effects held for people with moderate or little prior knowledge; the paper also compares text and visual presentation. Crowdsourced (Mechanical Turk) experiments. [abstract] — [UW Interactive Data Lab](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/explaining-the-gap); [paper PDF](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/files/2017-ExplainingTheGap-CHI.pdf)
- Follow-up: Hullman, Kay, Kim & Shrestha, "Imagining Replications", reports that graphical prediction improves recall and estimation of effect uncertainty. [abstract] — [paper PDF](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/files/2017-ImaginingReplications-InfoVis.pdf)
- Hullman, Adar & Shah (2011), "Benefitting InfoVis with Visual Difficulties": argues chart design trades off reading efficiency against learning, and that deliberate difficulty suits situations calling for active processing. A position paper, not an experiment. — [paper PDF](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/files/2011-VisualDifficulties-InfoVis.pdf)
- Linn et al. (2010) describe the "deceptive clarity" of visualisations: a clear display makes learners feel they understand, and desirable difficulties are proposed as the counter. [abstract] — [SRI listing](https://www.sri.com/publication/education-learning-pubs/stem-and-computer-science-pubs/can-desirable-difficulties-overcome-deceptive-clarity-in-scientific-visualizations/)
- The claim that interactive articles boost learning and engagement over static ones is made by Distill (2020) as a review-style assertion, not as a controlled result. [opinion/secondary] — [Distill](https://distill.pub/2020/communicating-with-interactive-articles/)

### Inferences
- Animate only what changes in the game: the range strip or map re-sorting from flop to turn is exactly the "change is the content" case where animation effects are larger. Decorative transitions have no supporting evidence.
- "Guess first" fits a phone well: before showing the solved chart, ask the player to drag a bar or tap where a hand sits, then show the gap. This has more direct evidence than free sliders.
- What-if sliders should come with a question to answer ("make the bet bigger: which hands stop calling?"). Guidance is where the simulation gains come from.
- A polished, instantly readable chart can create false confidence (deceptive clarity); a prediction step or a recall check is the documented countermeasure.

### Gaps
- No effect sizes retrieved for "Explaining the Gap"; whether the benefit lasts beyond the session is unknown from what I saw.
- I found no controlled study of "explorable explanations" as such showing a learning gain over a well-made static version. One small study (exploRNN, 37 participants) exists but its results were not retrieved.
- Learner-control (pacing, scrubbing) moderator values from the animation meta-analyses were not retrieved.
- de Jong's own papers on guided simulation were not retrieved; the guidance point rests on the two meta-analyses above.
- Brod (2021) review of generating predictions as a learning strategy: not found.

## 5. Signalling, labels in the chart versus legends, colour, progressive disclosure, small screens

### Takeaway
Putting words next to the marks they describe and cueing the important parts are two of the largest and most consistent effects in the multimedia literature, and both matter more on a small screen. Direct evidence on phone-sized learning graphics is thin and mixed.

### Cited Findings
- Spatial contiguity / split attention: Schroeder & Cenkci (2018), Educational Psychology Review 30(3), 679-701: 58 independent comparisons, n = 2,426, integrated designs over separated ones g = 0.63. [abstract/secondary] — [TUM Clearinghouse summary](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/CHU_KR-23_Schroeder-2018_Lernmaterial.pdf)
- Signalling: Schneider, Beege, Nebel & Rey (2018), Educational Research Review 23, 1-24: signalling improved retention (139 comparisons) and transfer (70 comparisons). [secondary] — [TUM Clearinghouse summary](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/CHU_KR-22_-Schneider_2017_Lernmaterial_Hervorhebungen.pdf)
- The pooled values usually quoted for that meta-analysis are retention g+ = 0.53 and transfer g+ = 0.33, with lower reported effort and difficulty. [secondary only; not confirmed against the paper] — [QIC-WD umbrella summary](https://www.qic-wd.org/sites/default/files/Umbrella%20Summary%20-%20Signaling%20in%20Training%20111021.pdf)
- A second cueing meta-analysis (Alpizar et al.) is quoted as: retention d = 0.27, transfer d = 0.34, cognitive load d = -0.11. The same summary gives inconsistent years for it. [secondary, low confidence] — [QIC-WD umbrella summary](https://www.qic-wd.org/sites/default/files/Umbrella%20Summary%20-%20Signaling%20in%20Training%20111021.pdf)
- Segmenting: Rey et al. (2019), Educational Psychology Review 31, 389-419: breaking material into learner-paced segments gives small to medium gains for retention and transfer. [abstract] — [MPI listing](https://is.tuebingen.mpg.de/publications/rey2018segmenting)
- Screen size, mixed single studies: one study found learners on medium and large phone screens learned significantly more from video than those on small screens — [Multimedia Tools and Applications](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11042-016-4062-2); another found video raised knowledge regardless of phone screen size — [University of Portsmouth listing](https://researchportal.port.ac.uk/en/publications/the-effect-of-mobile-screen-size-on-video-based-learning/); a chemistry-text study found no effect of screen size, orientation or text segmentation on outcomes — [UT Austin repository](https://repositories.lib.utexas.edu/handle/2152/30465). [all secondary]

### Inferences
- Label the strips, curve regions and map quadrants on the chart itself. A separate legend, or an explanation the player must scroll to, is the split-attention layout, and on a phone the two halves may not even be on screen together.
- Signalling is the cheap way to make plain-English explanations and charts work as one: when the text says "the top of his range", that part of the strip should highlight.
- Progressive disclosure is supported indirectly by the segmenting effect: reveal one layer per tap (one range, then the other, then the difference) rather than the full chart at once.
- The contiguity figure (g = 0.63) is larger than the animation figure (g = 0.23): integrating labels is likely worth more than adding motion.

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analysis of screen size or mobile learning with graphics; the single studies conflict and mostly concern video, not charts.
- No learning-outcome evidence retrieved on colour use (number of hues, redundant coding, colour-blind-safe palettes) in instructional graphics. Treat any colour guidance in the report as perception/design practice, not learning-science evidence.
- No direct study of "progressive disclosure" under that name was found.

## 6. Comparison and contrast in visual form

### Takeaway
Comparison is easier and more accurate when the two things are placed so their matching parts line up directly; which layout achieves this depends on the direction in which the visual is structured. This directly governs how two range strips should be arranged.

### Cited Findings
- Matlen, Gentner & Franconeri (2020), "Spatial alignment facilitates visual comparison", JEP: Human Perception and Performance 46(5), 443-457: the spatial alignment principle says comparison is best when visuals are placed perpendicular to their structural axes, so matching components are in direct alignment. Across four experiments direct placement gave faster and more accurate comparison than other placements of the same patterns. [abstract] — [paper PDF](https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/gentner/papers/MatlenEtAl_2020-SpatialAlign.pdf)
- Follow-ups: the advantage of direct over impeded placement also held for diagonal structures (2022 conference paper), for 6-year-olds, and was extended to seventh-grade life-science visuals, where direct placement may help with complex unfamiliar images. [secondary] — [Cognitive Science Society proceedings](https://escholarship.org/content/qt4zc3w6wf/qt4zc3w6wf.pdf?t=sgo63u)

### Inferences
- Rule for the strips: if each strip runs left to right (strong to weak), stack the two players' strips one above the other, so equal strength sits directly above and below. If strips run top to bottom, put them side by side. "Side by side" in the brief is only right for vertical strips. On a portrait phone, horizontal strips stacked vertically use the width well and satisfy the rule.
- Share the same axis, scale and sort order in both strips; anything that breaks the one-to-one correspondence turns direct placement into impeded placement.
- Minimal pairs (same hand range on two boards differing in one card) and small multiples follow the same logic: hold everything constant and aligned except the one thing being contrasted.

### Gaps
- Effect sizes and accuracy/response-time numbers from Matlen et al. were not retrievable.
- Gentner's structure-mapping papers, and learning-outcome studies of small multiples or minimal-pair diagrams, were not retrieved. The small-multiples recommendation is an inference from alignment research, not a tested learning result.
- The alignment experiments measure speed and accuracy of comparison, not later retention or transfer.

## 7. Learner-generated and completed diagrams

### Takeaway
Having the learner produce or complete a representation generally beats studying a finished one, with a moderate pooled advantage for constructing over studying, but novices often need a partly built scaffold to produce anything useful.

### Cited Findings
- Schroeder, Nesbit, Anguiano & Adesope (2018), Educational Psychology Review: 142 effect sizes, n = 11,814. Learning with concept/knowledge maps g = 0.58 overall; constructing maps g = 0.72 versus studying maps g = 0.43 (each against its own comparison conditions). [abstract] — [SFU Summit repository](https://summit.sfu.ca/item/17789)
- Ainsworth & Loizou (2003), Cognitive Science: learners studying the circulatory system while self-explaining did significantly better on post-tests with diagrams than with text, produced significantly more self-explanations with diagrams, and the benefit of self-explaining was much greater in the diagram condition. [abstract] — [paper listing](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/the-effects-of-self-explaining-when-learning-with-text-or-diagrams/812270780938190848-7932)
- A study on drawing and explaining found a draw-and-explain group produced higher-quality explanations than an explain-only group. [secondary] — [NSF PAR paper](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10348176)
- A 2023 review of generative learning cites Fiorella & Zhang (2018) for the point that some learners need heavy guidance before they can generate good representations. [secondary] — [Educational Psychology Review 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-023-09769-7)

### Inferences
- Charts are a better prompt for self-explanation than text. Pair each chart with one "why does this range look like this?" prompt before showing the written explanation, rather than leading with the explanation.
- Completion tasks suit a phone: show the strip with a segment blank, or the map with three hands missing, and let the player place them. This is generation with scaffolding, the form the guidance caveat points to.
- Free drawing of a range from nothing is the high-demand end and likely too hard for novices on a small touch screen.

### Gaps
- Nesbit & Adesope (2006) concept-map meta-analysis: not retrieved; the 2018 update above supersedes it.
- Cromley and colleagues on partially completed diagrams: the paper was located by citation but its partial-diagram manipulation and results could not be confirmed.
- Fiorella & Zhang (2018) drawing review: effect sizes not retrieved.
- Ainsworth & Loizou effect sizes and sample size not retrieved (I recall a small sample, unverified).

## 8. Graph literacy and introducing unfamiliar chart types

### Takeaway
An unfamiliar chart is a learning task in itself: novices must first build a "frame" for how it encodes data, and they can get stuck. Onboarding helps mainly for the genuinely unfamiliar chart, and the evidence that it helps much is modest and partly conflicting.

### Cited Findings
- Lee, Kim, Hung, Lam, Kang & Yi (2016), IEEE TVCG 22(1), 499-508: think-aloud study of novices facing a parallel-coordinates plot, a chord diagram and a treemap, analysed by grounded theory. The NOVIS model has five activities: encountering the visualisation, constructing a frame, exploring, questioning the frame, and floundering. Sample reported as 13 participants in the abstract (slides mention 34 sessions). [abstract/secondary] — [paper record](https://www.aminer.org/pub/5687e80c0cf2afdcd166006b/how-do-people-make-sense-of-unfamiliar-visualizations-a-grounded-model-of); [presentation slides](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~tmm/courses/547-15/slides/rex-novis.pdf)
- A 2024 interview study found students' sense-making followed many of the same steps before and after a visualisation course. [secondary] — [Northwestern paper](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/files/2024-vis-lit-interview.pdf)
- Stoiber et al. (2022), "Comparative Evaluations of Visualization Onboarding Methods", Visual Informatics 6(4): across four chart types onboarding did not measurably change answer correctness overall; the exception reported is the parallel-coordinates plot, the least familiar chart. Comparing a step-by-step guide, a scrollytelling tutorial and a video for that chart, participants rated the video best by sentiment; the authors conclude scrollytelling suits explaining the encoding and video suits explaining interactions. [abstract] — [arXiv 2203.11134](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11134); [TU Wien repository](https://repositum.tuwien.at/handle/20.500.12708/192830?mode=full)
- Conflict: a 2025 education-visualisation report describes the scrollytelling result as no statistically significant improvement in reading parallel coordinates. — [Andrews, EduVis 2025](https://ftp.isds.tugraz.at/pub/papers/andrews-eduvis2025-pcee.pdf); contradicts the reading of [Stoiber et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11134)

### Inferences
- The 13x13 grid is the audience's familiar chart; the strength-by-volatility map is the novel one and carries the largest "frame construction" cost. Its axes need to be taught explicitly, ideally by placing three or four well-known hands on it first.
- Derive new charts from the familiar one where possible ("these are the grid's hands, re-sorted by strength") so the player's existing frame carries over. This is DeFT's constrain function used as onboarding.
- Onboarding effort should be spent on the novel chart only; familiar forms showed no measurable benefit.
- Each new chart type has a real cost. Two new charts plus a shape vocabulary introduced together is three unfamiliar things at once; stagger them.

### Gaps
- Börner et al. on data visualisation literacy: not retrieved.
- Kwon & Lee (2016) on active-learning tutorials for parallel coordinates: not found; I recall it reporting that interactive tutorials beat static ones, unverified.
- No study quantifies the learning cost of a novel chart against a familiar one for the same content.

## 9. Expertise reversal and seductive details in visuals

### Takeaway
Interesting but irrelevant additions measurably reduce learning, more so for novices and when placed beside the relevant diagram. Support that helps novices can become redundant for experts, so explanatory layers should be dismissible; the second point is well known but I could not source it here.

### Cited Findings
- Sundararajan & Adesope (2020), "Keep it Coherent: A Meta-Analysis of the Seductive Details Effect", Educational Psychology Review 32(3), 707-734: including seductive details lowers learning outcomes; the harm is worse when the irrelevant material sits next to relevant diagrams or is static. Press accounts give 58 studies; another summary says 68 experiments. One author calls the effect fairly small. [secondary] — [WSU news release](https://news.wsu.edu/press-release/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning); [WSU repository record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Keep-it-Coherent-A-Meta-Analysis-of/99900601155501842)
- Novices appear more affected than learners with some prior knowledge; the effect is reported as considerable in short lessons and not significant in longer ones. [secondary, blog summary] — [Learning and the Brain](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/seductive-details-when-do-cool-stories-and-videos-interfere-with-learning/)
- Prior knowledge as a moderator of representation support is also visible in Rau's chemistry result (section 1), where only high-prior-knowledge students benefited. [secondary] — [Rau-lab paper via NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10421205)

### Inferences
- Keep card art, chip graphics, mascots and decorative animation out of the chart area; a short phone study session is the "short lesson" case where the harm was largest.
- Make labels and explanations collapsible so an experienced player can strip the chart down. This follows the expertise-reversal idea, flagged below as unsourced.

### Gaps
- The pooled effect size for seductive details was not retrieved.
- Expertise reversal (Kalyuga and colleagues) for diagrams with redundant text: no source retrieved in this session. I recall the standard finding that integrated explanatory text helps novices and adds load for experts, unverified here.
- Rey (2012) seductive-details meta-analysis: not retrieved.
