## Diagrammatic reasoning: do redesigned notations improve problem solving and learning?

### Takeaway
The theory is consistent across Larkin & Simon, Zhang & Norman, Scaife & Rogers and Cheng: two displays holding the same information can differ greatly in the work needed to use it, and a display helps when the rule or relation the task needs is carried by the geometry so it is perceived, not remembered or calculated. Controlled experiments by Cheng (electricity, probability) and by Böcherer-Linder & Eichler (Bayesian reasoning) report learning and accuracy gains for such redesigned diagrams, but the samples are students in instructional settings and I could not see effect sizes.

### Cited Findings
- Larkin & Simon (1987, Cognitive Science 11, 65-99) compare informationally equivalent diagrammatic and sentential representations. Whether a diagram is worth "10,000 words" depends on what productions are available for searching the data structure and recognising relevant information; diagrams "automatically support a large number of perceptual inferences, which are extremely easy for humans"; and "to be useful a diagram must be constructed to take advantage of these features". The advantage is attributed to indexing by location, shown with computational models, not with a human experiment. — [Larkin & Simon 1987 PDF mirror](https://mechanism.ucsd.edu/bill/teaching/F12/cs200/Readings/larkin.whyadiagramissometimesworth.1987.pdf); [summary](https://www.jimdavies.org/summaries/larkin1987.html)
- Zhang & Norman (1994, Cognitive Science 18(1), 87-122) treat a task as a system of internal and external representations that together represent its abstract structure. They built four sets of Tower of Hanoi isomorphs; the abstract-level result is that rules represented externally (built into the physical display, so illegal moves cannot be made or are visibly illegal) make the problem easier, with rule checking and discovery of recursive strategies contributing. — [Zhang & Norman 1994 PDF](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~scoulson/203/zhang.pdf); [record](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/representations-in-distributed-cognitive-tasks/812265364321730560-7932)
- Scaife & Rogers (1996, Int. J. Human-Computer Studies 45, 185-213) make "computational offloading" the central property of external representations: the extent to which different representations vary the cognitive effort a task needs. They break it into re-representation, graphical constraining and temporal constraining. Their review of the literature (centred on four representative studies) concludes that the account of how graphical representations work was fragmented and poorly understood. This is a framework and critique, not an experiment. — [preprint, Sussex CSRP 335](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/informatics/cogslib/reports/csrp/csrp335.pdf); [summary](https://edutechwiki.unige.ch/en/External_cognition)
- Cheng (2002, Cognitive Science 26(6), "Electrifying diagrams for learning") proposes Law Encoding Diagrams, with AVOW diagrams (amps, volts, ohms, watts drawn as the width, height, slope and area of rectangles) as the example. A learning experiment compared an AVOW group with an equations-taught group on recall, multiple-choice and transfer problems; the AVOW group showed larger learning gains, particularly on complex interaction problems and transfer problems. Population: advanced high-school students. — [paper](https://users.sussex.ac.uk/~peterch/papers/Cheng-CogSci26%286%29.pdf); [record](https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Electrifying_diagrams_for_learning_principles_for_complex_representational_systems/23347637)
- Cheng (2011, Topics in Cognitive Science, "Probably good diagrams for learning: representational epistemic recodification of probability theory") reports an experiment in which the new Probability Space diagrams were "superior to the conventional approach" for learning probability theory. — [published version](https://users.sussex.ac.uk/~peterch/papers/Cheng-TopiCS-2011.pdf); [preprint](https://users.sussex.ac.uk/~peterch/papers/ChengMS-ToPICS-preprint.pdf)
- Cheng & Pitt (2003, Mathematical Gazette) state that studies of instruction with Probability Space diagrams showed they "can significantly enhance students' conceptual understanding". — [article](https://users.sussex.ac.uk/~peterch/papers/ChengMathgazette.pdf)
- Böcherer-Linder & Eichler (2017, Frontiers in Psychology): the unit square (a square cut so that areas are joint probabilities) beat the tree diagram. Experiment 1 (148 students): better at quantifying the subset relation Bayes' rule needs. Experiment 2 (143 students): significantly better on Bayes' rule tasks. — [Frontiers 2016.02026](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.02026/text)
- Böcherer-Linder & Eichler (2019, Frontiers in Psychology; 688 undergraduates; 2x2 table, unit square, icon array, tree, double tree): no additional benefit from area-proportionality as such; a benefit from showing discrete countable objects; and a "strong facilitating effect" of making the nested-sets structure graphically transparent. — [Frontiers 2019.00267](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00267/pdf)
- Eichler, Böcherer-Linder & Vogel (2020): a tree diagram, compared with a unit square, partly hinders finding the correct denominator and triggers selecting a wrong numerator. The same paper puts typical correct-response rates for Bayesian tasks given a visualisation plus natural frequencies at about 40-70%. — [Frontiers 2020.01897](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.01897/pdf)
- Stull & Mayer (2007): across three experiments, students given author-provided graphic organisers did as well as or better than those who built or completed their own (Experiment 1 no significant difference; a later experiment favoured author-provided); the authors suggest construction imposes extra cognitive load. — [proceedings paper](https://escholarship.org/content/qt8x60f70g/qt8x60f70g.pdf?t=op2mr8)
- Kim, Reinecke & Hullman (CHI 2017, "Explaining the Gap"): participants who drew their prediction and self-explained before seeing the data recalled and understood the data better than controls; the benefit came most from seeing the gap between prediction and data, and was larger for visual than text conditions. — [paper](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/files/2017-ExplainingTheGap-CHI.pdf); [summary](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/explaining-the-gap)

### Inferences
- The 2019 Böcherer-Linder result is the most specific design guidance here for range-versus-range poker questions: what helped was making the part-whole (nested set) structure visible and the units countable, not area-proportionality alone. A chart that shows "of all hands, this slice is strong, and of that slice this part bets" as visibly nested regions matches the condition that helped.
- A 13x13 grid is informationally equivalent to a display sorted by hand strength, but in Larkin & Simon's terms not computationally equivalent for "who has more strong hands": the grid indexes by card rank, so answering needs a search plus a mental weighted sum (combo counts differ by cell: pairs 6, suited 4, offsuit 12), none of which the geometry carries. This is my application of the framework, not a tested result.
- Cheng's Law Encoding idea maps onto poker identities that are true by arithmetic (for example pot odds versus required equity, or a bluff-to-value ratio for a bet size): a diagram can make a strategy that violates the identity look geometrically wrong. No study tests this in a game domain.
- For retention, Kim et al. suggests a predict-then-reveal interaction (draw the range or betting frequency, then see the solver's) rather than passive viewing. Stull & Mayer cautions that making learners build the whole diagram themselves is not reliably better than giving them a good one.

### Gaps
- Effect sizes, sample sizes and retention delays for Cheng's experiments and step/time/error data for Zhang & Norman could not be read (hosts blocked). Cheng's studies are from one lab with small classroom-scale samples as far as I recall (unverified recall); I found no independent replication.
- Stenning & Oberlander (1995, Cognitive Science, specificity theory: graphics help because they limit what can be expressed, forcing a determinate model) was not retrieved; no cited finding is given. Unverified recall only.
- Zhang's "representational effect" definition (same abstract structure, different representations, very different behaviour) is unverified recall; the search result confirmed the framework but not that wording.
- No study was found on diagram redesign for game strategy or poker specifically.

## Graph perception: channel rankings, ensemble perception, comparison

### Takeaway
Position on a common scale is read most precisely and colour/shading least, and a crowdsourced replication largely confirmed that order; but the ranking is about extracting ratios between two marked values, and newer work disputes how far it generalises. The visual system does extract summary statistics from many marks in a fraction of a second, while comparing specific subsets is slow and attention-limited, so the designer has to arrange and group marks so that the wanted comparison is the one that falls out.

### Cited Findings
- Cleveland & McGill (1984, JASA) list ten elementary perceptual tasks; the commonly taught order is position on a common scale, position on non-aligned scales, length / direction / angle, area, volume / curvature, then shading and colour saturation. Summaries note the average accuracy differences between adjacent ranks were often small. Experiment 1 was position versus length (bar-chart variants); Experiment 2 was position versus angle (bar versus pie). — [course summary](https://info3312.infosci.cornell.edu/slides/04-graphical-perception.html); [secondary](https://vcg.seas.harvard.edu/publications/20181031-evaluating-graphical-perception-with-cnns)
- Heer & Bostock (CHI 2010) replicated the position-length-angle experiments on Mechanical Turk with the same task (estimate what percentage the smaller marked value is of the larger) and the same log absolute error measure, extended to circular and rectangular area, and found the ranking largely held. A later replication (Guo et al.) got slightly higher errors, probably because Heer & Bostock excluded responses more than 40 points off. — [paper page](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/crowdsourcing-graphical-perception); [replication note](https://diglib7.eg.org/bitstream/handle/10.2312/evs20201046/043-047.pdf)
- Contested: "The Risks of Ranking" argues the canonical order describes average performance and hides large individual differences, and other work finds it does not explain performance on discrimination tasks. — [arXiv 2212.10533](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.10533)
- Franconeri, Padilla, Shah, Zacks & Hullman (2021, Psychological Science in the Public Interest 22(3)): viewers extract global statistics (means and similar) within a fraction of a second, but comparisons between sets of values are slow; use visual grouping cues to steer the viewer to the intended comparison; avoid overloading working memory; follow familiar conventions; put labels in the graphic not a separate legend; the review ranks channels (position, length, area, angle, intensity) by precision of ratio judgements. — [APS page](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/visual-data-communication.html); [APS news](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/2021-pspi-visualize-data.html)
- Szafir, Haroz, Gleicher & Franconeri (2016, Journal of Vision 16(5):11) sort the statistical judgements people make from many marks into four types: identifying sets of values, summarising across values, segmenting collections, estimating structure. This is a review and taxonomy with open questions, not a single effect size. — [author copy](https://scholar.colorado.edu/downloads/bz60cw82n)
- Haroz & Whitney (2012, IEEE TVCG 18(12)): the capacity limits of attention strongly limit visualisation effectiveness, especially detecting unexpected information. Tasks were target presence, oddball detection and judging the number of categories, with colours grouped by similarity or randomly placed. Design guideline from the authors: grouping similar data aids spotting outliers and trends. (A secondary write-up says grouping made the oddball and category-count tasks substantially easier; not verified in the paper.) — [paper](https://whitneylab.berkeley.edu/PDFs/Haroz_Whitney_2012_InfoVis.pdf); [secondary](https://medium.com/visumd/attention-or-lack-thereof-to-detail-understanding-limits-of-visual-attention-span-7e54fe0db6b7)
- Jardine, Ondov, Elmqvist & Franconeri (2020, IEEE TVCG 26(1)): for set-level comparisons ("biggest mean", "biggest range"; earlier "biggest delta", "biggest correlation") precision varied widely with mark type and arrangement, and viewers appear to use "perceptual proxies" (simple visual features that stand in for the statistic), so the best layout depends on the comparison. — [paper](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10196698)
- Ondov et al. ("Revealing Perceptual Proxies with Adversarial Examples") tested candidate proxies by building charts designed to deceive a viewer relying on each proxy. — [paper](https://www.hcil.umd.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/Ondov2020Revealing.pdf)

### Inferences
- Colour intensity in a grid cell sits at the bottom of the precision ranking, so a grid coloured by action frequency supports "roughly where is the betting" (an ensemble judgement) but not "how often does this hand bet" or "is the total betting frequency 33% or 45%". Total betting frequency needs a mental sum over cells of unequal weight, which no ensemble mechanism does for the viewer. A single aligned bar or a position mark does.
- "Who holds more strong hands" is a set-versus-set comparison, the slow kind in Franconeri et al. It becomes fast only when each range is reduced to marks on a shared, aligned scale (two lengths side by side, or two curves on one axis), following the position-on-common-scale result.
- Because perceptual proxies differ by task, one chart is unlikely to serve all three poker questions; a chart per question (or a view that rearranges per question) is the more defensible reading.

### Gaps
- Per-channel error values from Cleveland & McGill and Heer & Bostock, and Heer & Bostock's findings on rectangle aspect ratio, chart size and gridlines, could not be read.
- Gleicher's comparison taxonomy (juxtaposition, superposition, explicit encoding of the difference; Gleicher et al. 2011) and Ondov et al. 2019 "Face to Face" (arrangement effects on comparison) were not retrieved. Unverified recall: superposed or adjacent aligned arrangements beat separated ones for most comparisons, and encoding the difference directly helps when the difference is the question.
- No experimental evidence on small multiples was retrieved in this session.
- Pre-attentive "pop-out" features (Treisman; Healey & Enns) were not searched.

## Graph comprehension models and task-display compatibility

### Takeaway
What a viewer concludes depends on the fit between display format and task: bars make people state discrete comparisons, lines make them state trends, and putting things close together or in one object helps integration but hurts reading single values. Salience helps only when the viewer already knows what matters, so design cannot substitute for domain knowledge but can amplify it.

### Cited Findings
- Zacks & Tversky (1999, Memory & Cognition 27(6), 1073-1079): in two experiments viewers described bar graphs as discrete comparisons between individual points and line graphs as continuous trends; in a third, people asked to draw a graph for a verbal description chose bars for discrete comparisons and lines for trends. The effect did not depend on explicit knowledge of rules. — [paper](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/bf03201236.pdf)
- Wickens & Carswell (1995, Human Factors 37(3), 473-494), proximity compatibility principle: tasks that need information integrated are served by displays that put the sources close together (space, colour, shared object); tasks needing focused attention on one value are served by separated displays. Wickens & Andre (1988) found an object display gave better integration performance than separate bars but worse focused-attention performance. Carswell's tests of 13 bivariate graph types generally fit the predictions, with benefits of heterogeneous object displays limited to one task. Not all tests supported the principle. — [citation record](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/13681165); [Carswell dissertation](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/69880); [object display](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/proximity-compatibility-and-the-object-display/811078699435687936-48672)
- Hegarty, Canham & Fabrikant (2010, JEP: Learning, Memory, and Cognition 36(1), 37-53): making task-relevant information more salient on weather maps had no effect on accuracy before participants were taught the relevant meteorology; after instruction, the salient-relevant maps gave higher accuracy. Effects on accuracy were only partly mirrored in eye fixations, which were driven mainly by task and knowledge. — [paper](https://typeset.io/pdf/thinking-about-the-weather-how-display-salience-and-2wgkcgr4gn.pdf)
- Shah & Hoeffner (2002, Educational Psychology Review 14(1), 47-69) review three factors that determine what is understood from a graph: its visual characteristics (format, colour, legend, size and so on), the viewer's knowledge of graphs, and the viewer's knowledge and expectations about the content. — [record](https://vufind.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c395t-28948e3640fb7cd19a2d049dcfabf29f6d0dc7e1e4d3ca66e07993dc478944543/Details)
- Shah, Mayer & Hegarty (1999, J. Educational Psychology 91, 690-702) describe graph interpretation as easy pattern perception and association plus harder, error-prone inferential processes, and test "signalling" redesigns of textbook graphs to guide the latter. — [record](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/903528251)
- Franconeri et al. (2021): direct attention, respect conventions, and give extra support to viewers with low domain knowledge, numeracy or working memory. — [APS page](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/visual-data-communication.html)

### Inferences
- The common thread (Pinker's and Carpenter & Shah's models, as summarised in the reviews above): a relation is "seen" only if it maps to a single visual chunk (a slope, a gap, a taller bar). Relations that span chunks must be computed. So each strategic question should be answered by one visible feature: a gap between two curves, one bar length, one boundary line.
- Hegarty's interaction implies a threshold line or highlighted region ("hands above this line continue") will help a player who already knows what continuing range means and do little for one who does not; pair the mark with a one-line statement of the rule.
- The proximity principle supports overlaying the two players' ranges on one axis for "who is stronger", and warns that the same overlay makes it harder to read one player's exact value, so detail should be available separately.
- Zacks & Tversky implies that drawing equity across hands as a connected line invites reading it as a trend along a continuum; if the intended message is "group A versus group B", bars or grouped blocks will produce that statement more reliably.

### Gaps
- Pinker (1990, "A theory of graph comprehension") and Carpenter & Shah (1998, JEP: Applied, eye-fixation model of repeated encode-interpret cycles) were not retrieved; nothing is cited from them directly. Unverified recall: Shah & Carpenter found viewers describe x-y trends of the plotted lines and rarely the relation carried by the legend variable, so which variable goes on the x axis decides what is noticed.
- The experimental outcomes of Shah, Mayer & Hegarty's redesigns could not be read.

## Showing distributions and frequencies

### Takeaway
Discrete, countable marks beat continuous curves for lay probability judgements: icon arrays raise accuracy for low- and high-numeracy readers, quantile dot plots give more precise probability estimates than density curves and near-optimal decisions, and frequency framing ("3 out of 10") beats percentages. Cumulative curves are a mixed case: they misled people about the mean in one classic study, yet supported incentivised threshold decisions nearly as well as dot plots in another.

### Cited Findings
- Galesic, Garcia-Retamero & Gigerenzer (2009, Health Psychology 28, 210-216): icon arrays increased accuracy of both low- and high-numeracy people, even when the numbers were already given in a transparent form; described as especially promising for risk reduction. Side effects: risks shown as icon arrays were judged less serious than the same risks as numbers, and larger arrays (1,000 versus 100 icons) made risks seem more serious and reductions larger. — [paper](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_2099767_5/component/file_2562291/MG_Using_2009.pdf)
- Garcia-Retamero & Galesic (2010, Medical Decision Making; Social Science & Medicine): icon arrays reduced denominator neglect and helped people take differing sample sizes into account. — [MDM](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0272989X10369000); [SSM record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:socmed:v:70:y:2010:i:7:p:1019-1025)
- Garcia-Retamero, Cokely and colleagues (2012, J. Behavioral Decision Making 25(4), 390-401): icon arrays improved accuracy and confidence more for people with high graph literacy than low. — [record](https://digitalcommons.mtu.edu/michigantech-p/3546)
- Franconeri et al. (2021): for lay audiences avoid error bars and show discrete examples; for risk, use absolute rates and frequencies ("3 out of 10") over percentages, especially for low numeracy. — [APS news](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/2021-pspi-visualize-data.html)
- Kay, Kola, Hullman & Munson (CHI 2016, "When(ish) is My Bus?") introduced quantile dot plots (a distribution drawn as a small fixed number of dots, each an equal slice of probability, so a probability is read by counting). In a controlled experiment they reduced the variance of probability estimates by about 1.15 times relative to density plots. — [summary](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/when-ish-is-my-bus); [paper](https://vis.mit.edu/classes/6.859/readings/pdfs/Kay-WhenishIsMyBus.pdf)
- Fernandes, Walls, Munson, Hullman & Kay (CHI 2018): in an incentivised repeated decision task, 50-dot quantile dot plots gave expected payoffs 97% of optimal (95% CI 95-98%), 5 percentage points above a no-uncertainty control (95% CI 2-8), with lower within-subject variability. CDF plots performed nearly as well; textual uncertainty did worse and was sensitive to which interval was stated. — [summary](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/uncertainty-bus); [paper](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/files/2018-UncertainBusDecisions-CHI.pdf)
- Ibrekk & Morgan (1987, Risk Analysis 7(4), 519-529) tested nine displays of an uncertain quantity on well-educated semi-technical and non-technical people for three tasks (the mean, probability above a threshold, probability within a range). CDFs alone "can severely mislead some subjects in estimating the mean"; rusty statistics knowledge did not help. Provisional recommendation: a CDF directly above a density on the same horizontal scale with the mean marked on both. — [record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:riskan:v:7:y:1987:i:4:p:519-529)
- Hullman, Resnick & Adar (2015, PLOS ONE): hypothetical outcome plots (animated individual draws) gave similar accuracy to error bars and violin plots for questions about one distribution, but much more accurate judgements for questions about two and three quantities, such as how often one exceeds another. — [summary](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/project/hops); [record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:plo:pone00:0142444)
- Kale, Nguyen, Kay & Hullman (2019, IEEE TVCG): with HOPs, untrained observers could correctly infer the underlying trend at lower levels of evidence than with error bars or line ensembles. — [summary](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/hops-trends)
- Correll & Gleicher (2014, IEEE TVCG 20(12), 2142-2151): the encoding of mean and error significantly changes decisions; they recommend gradient plots and violin plots over bars with error bars for inferential tasks. — [paper](https://graphics.cs.wisc.edu/Papers/2014/CG14/Error%20Bars%20Considered%20Harmful%20Exploring%20Alternate%20Encodings%20for%20Mean%20and%20Error.pdf)
- Newman & Scholl (2012, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review 19(4), 601-607), "within-the-bar bias": when a bar shows a mean, viewers judge values inside the bar as more likely than equally distant values outside it; held with and without error bars and across several populations. Okan et al. (2018) found it affected health decisions. — [record](https://www.sci.utah.edu/~kpotter/Library/Papers/newman:2012:WTBB/index.html); [Okan 2018](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_3037565_7/component/file_3569416/Okan_Biasing_2018_VerlPDF_final_LS.pdf)

### Inferences
- A poker equity distribution curve is a quantile function or CDF variant. Ibrekk & Morgan and Fernandes et al. together suggest it is good for one job, threshold questions ("what share of the range has more than 50% equity"), and poor for another, judging the average or the overall shape. Marking the threshold and the mean on the curve addresses the documented failure.
- A range is naturally a set of countable combos, so it fits the icon-array and quantile-dot-plot evidence well: a range drawn as, say, 50 or 100 equal-weight dots sorted by strength lets "how often to bet" be read as a count of coloured dots and "who has more strong hands" as two counts above a line. The evidence is from transit and medical risk; transfer to poker is my inference.
- "How often does my hand beat theirs" is a two-quantity comparison, the case where HOPs clearly beat static summaries; a static equivalent is a paired or mirrored dot plot on one strength axis.
- Avoid bars for average equity of a range: the within-the-bar bias would make values below the mean look more likely than values above.

### Gaps
- Percent-correct figures by numeracy group in Galesic et al. 2009 could not be read.
- Gigerenzer & Hoffrage (1995) on natural frequencies was not retrieved directly; the benefit is contested in size and explanation (nested-sets account versus evolutionary frequency account), consistent with the Böcherer-Linder 2019 finding above that nested-set transparency is what helps.
- Which dot count is best: unverified recall is that Kay et al. found a low-density plot (about 20 dots) best for fast estimates and Fernandes et al. used 20 and 50; only the 50-dot result is confirmed above.
- No study was found that directly tests comparing two overlaid distributions (two CDFs or two densities) for a "which is stronger" judgement in lay readers.

## Heatmaps and matrices

### Takeaway
Direct evidence on reading colour-coded grids is thin, and what exists says colour alone is weak for values: adding digits to cells raised accuracy by 10 to 60 percentage points, and palette choice changes estimation accuracy. The claim that sorting rows and columns by a meaningful variable improves pattern detection is a long-standing design principle (Bertin) with a large algorithm literature, but I found no controlled human study measuring the benefit.

### Cited Findings
- Han & Nacenta (Graphics Interface 2020) compared plain colour heatmaps, tables of digits, tooltips and digits overlaid on colour. Overlaying digits on colour improved accuracy by 10 to 60 percentage points across locating values, finding extrema and clustering tasks. The tooltip had a poor speed-accuracy trade-off, though participants preferred it to plain heatmap or digits only. — [paper](https://graphicsinterface.org/proceedings/gi2020/gi2020-23/)
- Molina, Middel & Vázquez (EuroVis 2023 short paper) found palette choice (Blues versus Viridis) affected value estimation and value search in heatmaps; clusters, outliers and trends were out of scope. — [paper](https://diglib.eg.org/items/bc1c1255-3957-4e09-b980-21b84af1db29/full)
- Behrisch, Bach, Henry Riche, Schreck & Fekete (2016, Computer Graphics Forum 35(3), 693-716) survey algorithms for reordering matrix rows and columns from statistics, bioinformatics and graph theory, with guidance on choosing by matrix structure and size. It is an algorithm survey, not a user study. — [paper](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.1111/cgf12935); [companion site](https://matrixreordering.dbvis.de)
- Benchmarks with synthetic matrices containing known patterns show reordering algorithms often fail to recover a good ordering when noise is present; this is algorithmic, not human-subject, evidence. — [Baroni & Silva comparative record](https://ersearch2.cvtisr.sk/vufind/EdsRecord/edsair,edsair.doi...........ad12de59e237f5158e83170729cf770f/UserComments)
- Ghoniem, Fekete & Castagliola (InfoVis 2004; Information Visualization 4(2), 2005): for graphs above about twenty vertices, a matrix view beat node-link diagrams on most of seven tasks, except path finding. A later large online study (Ren et al., Network Science) found node-link better understood than two matrix views. Matrices are for network data here, so relevance to a value grid is indirect. — [InfoVis 2004 record](https://www.lisn.upsaclay.fr/publications/a-comparison-of-the-readability-of-graphs-using-node-link-and-matrix-based-representations/); [Ren et al.](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/network-science/article/understanding-nodelink-and-matrix-visualizations-of-networks-a-largescale-online-experiment/C293136C4761F09D4C2C328E2BC19287)
- Haroz & Whitney (2012): spatially grouping same-coloured items, versus scattering them, is the manipulation their attention-limit experiments tested, and their guideline is that grouping aids outlier and trend detection. — [paper](https://whitneylab.berkeley.edu/PDFs/Haroz_Whitney_2012_InfoVis.pdf)

### Inferences
- The 13x13 poker grid has a fixed order (rank by rank, suited above the diagonal, offsuit below). That order is good for lookup by hand name and is learned by players, which matters (convention has value per Franconeri et al.). But it is only loosely ordered by the variable strategic questions depend on (strength against the opponent's range on this board), so colours that belong together are scattered, the condition Haroz & Whitney found harder.
- A view re-sorted by equity or by solver action would group like-coloured cells and turn "which hands continue" into one visible boundary. This follows from Bertin's principle and the grouping result, but the size of any benefit is not established by a user study I could find. It should be presented as a reasoned bet, not a proven effect.
- Cells in the grid are equal-sized but stand for unequal numbers of combos, so the area of a colour misstates its share of the range. That is a concrete, checkable flaw independent of the ordering question.
- Since colour-only cells are weak for values, any grid kept for lookup should carry digits or a second, positional encoding (for example a fill height in the cell) for frequency.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed human study was found that measures pattern detection in a sorted versus unsorted value matrix. This is the weakest-evidenced claim in the brief.
- Bertin's "Semiology of Graphics" (book, 1967/1983) and his reorderable matrix were not retrieved; it is a book of design argument, not experiment.
- No study of the poker hand grid as a visualisation was found.
- Simultaneous contrast effects on reading a cell's colour next to different neighbours (covered in Ware's "Information Visualization", a book) were not retrieved.

## Known failures and individual differences

### Takeaway
Charts mislead in systematic ways: designers assume viewers see the pattern they themselves see, salient spatial features override the labelled values for low-graph-literacy readers, and visual aids help the already-skilled more. Any "the chart implies the answer" design should be tested on people who do not already know the answer.

### Cited Findings
- Xiong, van Weelden & Franconeri (2020, IEEE TVCG 26(10)), curse of knowledge: participants given a backstory that made one pattern in a polling graph salient predicted that naive viewers would find the same pattern most salient, despite explicit instruction to ignore their own knowledge; annotations amplified the effect in follow-up experiments. — [paper](https://visualcognition.psych.northwestern.edu/publications/XiongCurse2020.pdf)
- Okan, Garcia-Retamero, Galesic & Cokely (2012, Spatial Cognition and Computation, "When higher bars are not larger quantities"): when a graph's spatial features conflict with its conventions (axis labels, scales), people with low graph literacy rely more on the salient spatial feature such as bar height; graph literacy predicts lower reliance on salient but non-diagnostic features. — [later summary](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_3037565_7/component/file_3569416/Okan_Biasing_2018_VerlPDF_final_LS.pdf); [background](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_literacy)
- Icon arrays raised accuracy and confidence more among high-graph-literacy participants than low. — [record](https://digitalcommons.mtu.edu/michigantech-p/3546)
- Hegarty et al. (2010): salience of the relevant feature gave no accuracy benefit before domain instruction. — [paper](https://typeset.io/pdf/thinking-about-the-weather-how-display-salience-and-2wgkcgr4gn.pdf)
- Within-the-bar bias and misreading of error bars at a basic level (Newman & Scholl 2012; Correll & Gleicher 2014). — [Newman & Scholl record](https://www.sci.utah.edu/~kpotter/Library/Papers/newman:2012:WTBB/index.html); [Correll & Gleicher](https://graphics.cs.wisc.edu/Papers/2014/CG14/Error%20Bars%20Considered%20Harmful%20Exploring%20Alternate%20Encodings%20for%20Mean%20and%20Error.pdf)
- Icon array size changes perceived seriousness of the same risk (1,000 versus 100 icons), so a display choice can shift judgement without changing the data. — [Galesic et al. 2009](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_2099767_5/component/file_2562291/MG_Using_2009.pdf)
- Franconeri et al. (2021): axis choices can hide real patterns or suggest false ones (a zero baseline is not always right); viewers with low domain knowledge, numeracy or working memory need more support. — [APS news](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/2021-pspi-visualize-data.html)
- Channel-precision rankings describe averages and hide large differences between individuals. — [arXiv 2212.10533](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.10533)

### Inferences
- The owner, as an expert, is exposed to the curse of knowledge: a chart that "obviously implies" an answer to them may not to a learner. The cheap check is to show a candidate chart to a weaker player with no caption and ask what they conclude.
- A chart that makes one answer vivid can produce overconfidence in a solver output that is itself approximate or board-specific. A chart that implies an answer should also show how much the answer moves (for example across similar boards), or learners may over-generalise a single picture.
- Novel diagrams carry a learning cost that the familiar grid does not; the gains in Cheng's studies came after instruction in the new notation. Expect a redesigned chart to need a short taught introduction before it outperforms the grid.

### Gaps
- No direct evidence was retrieved that a visualisation raised confidence without raising accuracy (overconfidence proper); the items above show bias and misreading, not calibration.
- Spatial ability as a moderator was not retrieved. Unverified recall: Hegarty and colleagues report that low-spatial viewers benefit less from, or are hurt by, displays needing mental transformation.
- The "illusion of causality" result (Xiong et al., bar charts prompting causal readings more than scatterplots) was searched for and not found in results; unverified recall only.
- Tufte's and Kosslyn's guidance (books) was not consulted; nothing here rests on them.
