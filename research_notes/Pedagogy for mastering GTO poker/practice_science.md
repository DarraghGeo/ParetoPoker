# Practice science: how practice should be scheduled and structured (and what it means for a GTO poker trainer)

Reader's note on evidence quality. Effect sizes below are quoted as the sources retrieved gave them. Where a number came from a secondary summary rather than the paper itself, it is marked "(secondary)". Most of this literature was built on verbal/factual materials, school mathematics, perceptual categories and motor skills; no peer-reviewed study of practice scheduling in poker was found, so every poker application sits under "Inferences", not "Cited Findings".

## 1. What are the best-supported findings and their sizes?

### Takeaway
Retrieval practice (testing) and distributed (spaced) practice are the two techniques with the strongest and broadest support (testing vs restudy about g = 0.5-0.7; larger with feedback); interleaving is a moderate, material-dependent effect (overall g = 0.42, ranging from +0.67 to -0.39 by material); feedback helps on average (d about 0.4-0.5) but with huge variance, and explanatory feedback beats right/wrong feedback by a wide margin. Learners systematically misjudge all of these: the conditions that feel best during practice (massing, blocking, re-reading, immediate feedback) are usually not the ones that produce the best delayed performance.

### Cited Findings

Overall rankings
- Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013, Psychological Science in the Public Interest) rated ten techniques. High utility: practice testing and distributed practice. Moderate: interleaved practice, elaborative interrogation, self-explanation. Low: summarization, highlighting/underlining, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text, rereading. — [APS release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html); [Dunlosky, American Educator 2013](https://www.aft.org/ae/fall2013/dunlosky)
- Donoghue & Hattie (2021) meta-analysed the same ten techniques: 242 studies, 1,619 effects, 169,179 participants, overall mean d = 0.56; distributed practice and practice testing again ranked top two. The authors caution that most studies used surface/factual outcomes, so application to deeper learning needs care. — [Donoghue & Hattie 2021, Frontiers in Education](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216/full)

Retrieval practice / testing effect
- Rowland (2014, Psychological Bulletin) meta-analysis of testing vs restudy: overall g = 0.50, 95% CI [0.42, 0.58]. — [cited in Pan & Sana 2021](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PS_2021.pdf)
- Rowland's feedback moderator: g = 0.73 with feedback vs g = 0.39 without (secondary; not checked against the original). Feedback is one of the reliable moderators of the testing effect. — [secondary summary](https://studycardsai.com/blog/active-recall-statistics); [review noting feedback as a reliable moderator](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6288371)
- Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017, Review of Educational Research 87(3), 659-701): practice tests beat restudy and all other comparison conditions; reported effect sizes vary by summary (g = 0.61 across 272 effects in one, g = 0.70 in another; the two may refer to different comparison sets). The effect was larger when practice and final test formats matched. — [Learning Scientists summary](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2017/2/9-1); [Adesope et al. PDF](https://education.wsu.edu/documents/2018/01/rethinking-use-tests.pdf/)
- Karpicke & Roediger (2008, Science 319, 966-968): repeated retrieval, not repeated study, drove long-term retention; students' predictions of their own later performance were uncorrelated with actual performance. — [Karpicke & Roediger 2008 PDF](https://www.brucehayes.org/Teaching/papers/2008_Roediger_Karpicke_Science.pdf)

Spacing / distributed practice
- Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006, Psychological Bulletin): 839 assessments of distributed practice in 317 experiments from 184 articles; the inter-study interval producing maximal retention increased as the retention interval increased. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/)
- Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler (2008, Psychological Science 19(11)): 1,350+ participants, gaps up to 3.5 months, tests up to 1 year later. Performance first rises then slowly falls as the gap grows; the optimal gap was about 20% of the test delay for delays of a few weeks, falling to about 5% for a 1-year delay. The cost of too-short a gap is much larger than the cost of too-long a gap (the curve rises steeply and declines gradually). — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/); [full text](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf)
- Expanding vs equal intervals: Landauer & Bjork (1978) argued for expanding intervals, but later work often found no advantage. Kang, Lindsey, Mozer & Pashler (2014) ran multi-day schedules and found expanding and equal-interval retrieval produced equivalent recall 8 weeks later. — [Kang et al. 2014](https://home.cs.colorado.edu/~mozer/Research/Selected%20Publications/reprints/KangLindseyMozerPashler2014.pdf)
- Successive relearning (Rawson & Dunlosky): retrieval practice with feedback until a criterion (e.g. one correct recall) is met, repeated across several spaced sessions. In one course study it raised exam performance by roughly a letter grade (C to B) versus spaced restudy and baseline (secondary summary of Rawson, Dunlosky & Sciartelli 2013). — [SecEd guide](https://www.sec-ed.co.uk/content/best-practice/a-teacher-s-guide-to-retrieval-practice-successive-relearning)

Interleaving vs blocking
- Brunmair & Richter (2019, Psychological Bulletin, "Similarity matters"): 59 studies, 238 effect sizes, 158 samples; overall g = 0.42 in favour of interleaving. Larger benefit when categories are more similar to each other (high between-category similarity), when items within a category are less similar, and for more complex materials. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/)
- By material: paintings g = 0.67 (largest); mathematics small but significant; expository texts g = 0.21 (not significant); words g = -0.39 (blocking better). — [Brunmair & Richter manuscript](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf); [secondary citation of text value](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PRKL_2024.pdf)
- Kornell & Bjork (2008, Psychological Science 19(6)): learning 12 painters' styles from examples; spaced/interleaved presentation beat massed presentation for classifying new paintings, yet participants rated massing as more effective even after seeing their own results. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18578849/); [PDF](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Kornell_Bjork_2008_PsychScience.pdf)
- A later summary of those experiments: more than 80% of participants rated massing as equally or more effective, while only 15% actually performed better with massing (secondary). — [Castel lab paper](https://castel.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/111/2016/11/SpacingInduction.pdf)
- Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung (2020, Journal of Educational Psychology 112(1), 40-52): cluster RCT, 54 seventh-grade classes, 787 students. On an unannounced test one month later the interleaved group scored 61% vs 38% for blocked, d = 0.83, 95% CI [0.68, 0.97]. Caveats: one district; every teacher reported that interleaved assignments took more time. — [What Works Clearinghouse review](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Study/88770); [paper PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2019-rohrer.pdf)

Desirable difficulties and learning vs performance
- Soderstrom & Bjork (2015, Perspectives on Psychological Science 10(2), 176-199): performance during practice is an unreliable index of learning. Gains in practice performance can fail to produce learning, some manipulations have opposite effects on performance and learning, and people mistake their performance during acquisition for a guide to long-term learning. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25910388/)

Variability of practice / contextual interference (motor-skill literature)
- Brady (2004) meta-analysis of contextual interference (random vs blocked practice order): 139 effect sizes from 61 studies; effect .57 in basic (laboratory) research but only .19 in applied research. — [Brady 2004 PDF](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/2004-brady.pdf)
- Czyz et al. (2024) meta-analysis of transfer: random over blocked SMD = 0.55 overall; 0.75 in laboratory settings (significant) vs 0.34 in applied settings (not significant); negligible in young participants (0.12). A companion paper on retention found the same pattern (medium overall, negligible in applied settings and the young). — [Czyz et al. 2024, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11349744/)

Feedback
- Kluger & DeNisi (1996, Psychological Bulletin 119, 254-284): 607 effect sizes, 23,663 observations; feedback interventions improved performance on average (d = 0.41), but more than one third of them decreased performance. — [SWOV record](https://swov.nl/nl/publicatie/effects-feedback-interventions-performance-historical-review-meta-analysis-and); [paper PDF](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Marking%20and%20Feedback/The%20effects%20of%20feedback%20interventions.pdf)
- Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020): 435 studies, k = 994, N > 61,000; d = 0.48 (random effects) with large heterogeneity. The information content of the feedback strongly moderates the effect, and feedback has more impact on cognitive and motor-skill outcomes than on motivational/behavioural outcomes. This is a marked reduction from the d = 0.79 often quoted from Hattie's earlier syntheses. — [Wisniewski et al. 2020, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/full)
- Van der Kleij, Feskens & Eggen (2015, Review of Educational Research): computer-based feedback, 70 effect sizes from 40 studies (range -0.78 to 2.29). Elaborated feedback (an explanation) = 0.49; knowledge of correct response (shows the answer) = 0.32; knowledge of results (right/wrong only) = 0.05. Elaborated feedback was especially better for higher-order outcomes. Immediate feedback looked better for lower-order learning, but that interaction was not significant. — [Van der Kleij et al. 2015 record](https://scinapse.io/papers/2110661236)
- Mertens, Finn & Lindner (2022) network meta-analysis reached the same ordering: elaborated feedback most likely best for both lower-order and transfer outcomes; right/wrong-only feedback least effective. — [IPN record](https://www.leibniz-ipn.de/en/research/publications/effects-of-computer-based-feedback-on-lower-and-higher-order-learning-outcomes)
- Feedback timing is contested. Kulik & Kulik (1988), 53 studies: in lab-style studies with a delayed test, delayed feedback won in all but one of 8 studies with 5-7 day retention; in applied classroom and computer-based instruction studies immediate feedback tended to win (also Azevedo & Bernard 1995). — [Smith & Kimball 2010 manuscript](https://www.ou.edu/memorylab/pdfs/SmithKimball_2010_LearningFromFeedback_ms.pdf)
- Mullet, Butler, Verdin, von Borries & Marsh (2014): in an upper-level engineering course, feedback on homework delayed by a week produced better performance on exam problems that were new but on the same concepts; students nevertheless said immediate feedback helped them most. — [WUSTL record](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/delaying-feedback-promotes-transfer-of-knowledge-despite-student-/)
- Caveat on that study: "immediate" meant available after the deadline; students actually viewed it on average 4 days after doing the homework vs 12 days in the delayed condition. So the study compares two delays and is partly a spacing manipulation, not a test of instant feedback. — [Learning Scientists](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2023/3/2)

Calibration, confidence and the illusion of competence
- Rereading is rated low utility; after restudy, learners judge items as more likely to be remembered, but actually remember more of the items they were tested on. — [Tullis, Finley & Benjamin 2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-012-0274-5); [APS release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)
- Hypercorrection (Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001, 2006): errors made with high confidence are more likely to be corrected after feedback than low-confidence errors; surprising feedback captures attention. Replicated by several groups. A rival explanation is that confidence in an error correlates with domain knowledge. — [Butterfield & Metcalfe 2006](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11409-006-6894-z); [review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4084803)
- Correct answers given with low confidence are fragile: without feedback, the learner is likely to answer wrongly later (Butler, Karpicke & Roediger 2008, as cited). Implication: feedback is worth giving on correct answers too when confidence was low. — [review citing Butler et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3970786)
- Calibration can be shifted by feedback, with limits: performance feedback reduced overconfidence while "environmental" feedback improved discrimination, and neither improved the other component (Stone & Opel 2000, as summarised). Feedback about overconfidence alone lowered confidence without improving calibration or resolution (Bornstein & Zickafoose, as summarised). In a classroom study, overconfidence fell and exam performance rose only for students who got feedback on both their performance and their judgements (Callender et al.). — [Callender, Franco-Watkins & Roberts 2016](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11409-015-9142-6); [calibration training paper](https://home.csulb.edu/~cwallis/382/certainty/overconfidence/Training%20to%20Improve%20Calibration.pdf)

### Inferences
- A drill that asks the player to commit to an answer before seeing the solver output is retrieval practice; browsing solver charts or watching a solution video is the analogue of rereading and should be expected to produce an illusion of competence (high felt fluency, weak delayed performance). The trainer should make "answer first, then look" the default path.
- The Van der Kleij ordering (0.05 / 0.32 / 0.49) is the single most actionable number for a trainer: a bare "correct / incorrect" (or an EV-loss number alone) is worth close to nothing; showing the correct strategy is worth a moderate amount; showing why is worth the most, especially for transfer to unseen spots.
- Because learners prefer massing, blocking and instant gratification even after seeing contrary results, the trainer should not rely on user self-report ("this mode feels effective") or within-session accuracy to choose its defaults. Track delayed accuracy (same spot class, days later) as the real learning metric.
- Session accuracy will be lower under spaced, interleaved, retrieval-heavy drilling. The tool should explain this to the user, otherwise the better mode looks like the worse one.

### Gaps
- Bjork's original "desirable difficulties" paper (1994) and Shute (2008, "Focus on formative feedback") and Hattie & Timperley (2007) were not fetched directly; their content is represented here only through later meta-analyses that update them. Hattie & Timperley's task / process / self-regulation / self levels of feedback are from background knowledge and should be checked before being quoted.
- Roediger & Karpicke (2006) specific numbers (restudy better at 5 minutes, testing better at 1 week) were not retrieved; that crossover pattern is widely reported but is not sourced here.
- Exact subgroup tables of Brunmair & Richter and Adesope et al. were not verified against the originals; the Adesope overall g is reported inconsistently across summaries.
- No meta-analysis of calibration training or of the hard-easy effect was found in this pass.

## 2. Where do they fail or reverse?

### Takeaway
Each technique has real boundary conditions: testing needs feedback or reasonable success rates and its advantage on highly interconnected material is disputed; interleaving reverses for dissimilar, easily told-apart categories and is weak before the learner knows the individual procedures; contextual-interference benefits shrink sharply outside the lab; feedback harms in more than a third of studies, particularly when it carries little information or draws attention to the self rather than the task; and novices generally need more guidance (worked examples) than experts.

### Cited Findings
- Testing with complex material (contested). Van Gog & Sweller (2015, Educational Psychology Review) argued the testing effect decreases or disappears as the element interactivity (interdependence of ideas) of the material rises. — [van Gog & Sweller 2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-015-9310-x)
- The rebuttal: Karpicke & Aue (2015) say that claim is wrong, that the review omitted studies showing retrieval effects with complex materials (including ones that manipulated complexity directly), and that element interactivity was never measured or manipulated, only judged subjectively. Rawson's commentary adds that the proposed moderator is not troublesome when the goal is long-term retention. — [Karpicke & Aue 2015](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-015-9309-3); [secondary summary of the exchange](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zMjGz9ddgwjxadBRxQCnnWY)
- Testing without feedback is weaker: g = 0.39 without vs 0.73 with feedback (secondary figures from Rowland 2014). — [secondary summary](https://studycardsai.com/blog/active-recall-statistics)
- Interleaving reverses with category structure. Carvalho & Goldstone (2014, Memory & Cognition): interleaving improved generalisation when categories were highly similar (hard to tell apart); blocking was better when categories had low similarity (the hard part is seeing what members share). — [Carvalho & Goldstone 2014](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13421-013-0371-0.pdf)
- The benefit of interleaving comes mainly from the cross-category comparison it allows, not from the temporal spacing it adds. — [Carvalho & Goldstone 2014, Frontiers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4141442)
- Interleaving reversed for word materials (g = -0.39) and was not significant for expository text (g = 0.21). — [Brunmair & Richter manuscript](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)
- Interleaving costs time: every teacher in the Rohrer et al. RCT reported interleaved assignments took longer. — [paper PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2019-rohrer.pdf)
- Random/varied practice order: medium effects in the lab, small or non-significant in applied settings (Brady: .57 vs .19; Czyz: 0.75 vs 0.34 n.s.), negligible in young learners. — [Brady 2004](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/2004-brady.pdf); [Czyz et al. 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11349744/)
- Spacing can be overdone: for a fixed test date, performance declines (gradually) once the gap exceeds the optimum. — [Cepeda et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/)
- Feedback harms in over one third of interventions (Kluger & DeNisi 1996). — [SWOV record](https://swov.nl/nl/publicatie/effects-feedback-interventions-performance-historical-review-meta-analysis-and)
- Right/wrong-only feedback is near zero (0.05) in computer-based settings. — [Van der Kleij et al. 2015 record](https://scinapse.io/papers/2110661236)
- Novices vs experts. Worked examples help learners with little domain knowledge and lose value for learners with substantial prior knowledge (expertise reversal; Kalyuga 2007 is the standard review). In a Cognitive Tutor, adaptively fading worked examples beat fixed fading, which beat pure problem solving, in both a lab and a classroom experiment (Salden et al. 2010). — [Salden et al. 2010](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11251-009-9107-8)
- The opposite sequencing also has support for conceptual learning: Sinha & Kapur (2021, Review of Educational Research), 53 studies and 166 comparisons, found problem-solving-before-instruction beat instruction-first by g = 0.36 [0.20, 0.51] (0.37-0.58 when productive-failure design principles were followed; 0.87 after a publication-bias adjustment, which is the less conservative figure). For procedural content, instruction-first worked as well. — [Sinha & Kapur 2021 record](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1406031499744456704); [press coverage with author comments](https://techlearning.com/news/study-productive-failure-a-success-in-education)
- Meta-analytic evidence for study techniques is dominated by surface/factual outcomes. — [Donoghue & Hattie 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216/full)

### Inferences
- Poker spots are high element-interactivity material (the right action depends on range composition, board texture, stack depth, position and sizing together). The van Gog & Sweller dispute matters here: the safe reading is that retrieval practice still works, but a novice who cannot yet represent the spot will gain little from being quizzed on it cold. Sequence should be: short explanation or worked example of the principle, then retrieval drills, with the worked-example share fading as accuracy rises (Salden et al.).
- Whether to block or interleave depends on what is hard. If the difficulty is discrimination (two board textures or hand classes that look alike but play differently, e.g. which of two similar flops is bet at high frequency), interleave them and ideally show them side by side. If the difficulty is finding the commonality (what all "range-bet" boards share), a short blocked run of that one category is defensible first, followed by interleaving.
- Mixing wholly unrelated spot families (preflop charts with river bluff-catching) gives spacing but not the discriminative-contrast benefit, and adds switching cost; the evidence supports mixing confusable spots more strongly than mixing everything.
- Kluger & DeNisi's finding that feedback shifting attention to the self hurts (their feedback intervention theory; mechanism from background knowledge, not verified in this pass) argues against leaderboards, "you are a fish"-style ratings and heavy score framing as the primary feedback; keep feedback on the task and the reasoning.
- An "attempt first, then explanation" order is supported both by the testing literature and by productive-failure results for conceptual content, but pure discovery without the follow-up explanation is not.

### Gaps
- No study was found that measures element interactivity quantitatively, so "complex" remains a judgement call.
- Kalyuga (2007) itself was not retrieved; expertise reversal effect sizes are not given here.
- Pretesting/errorful generation literature (e.g. Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009; Pan & Sana 2021) was not examined in detail; it is relevant to whether guessing a solver frequency before being taught is useful.
- Kluger & DeNisi's specific moderators (task vs self focus, task complexity) were not verified from the full text.

## 3. Learning category structure and rules from examples (inductive learning)

### Takeaway
People learn "what kind of thing is this" from examples best when confusable categories are juxtaposed (interleaved or shown side by side) so that the distinguishing features stand out; when categories are easy to tell apart and the challenge is seeing what members share, blocking is better. Learners reliably believe blocking is teaching them more.

### Cited Findings
- Kornell & Bjork (2008): interleaving artists' paintings improved classification of new paintings by the same artists; the proposed mechanism is that interleaving helps learners see the differences between categories. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18578849/)
- Carvalho & Goldstone (2014): schedule interacts with category structure; high within- and between-category similarity favours interleaving, low similarity favours blocking. Presenting items simultaneously helped high-similarity categories; low-similarity categories still showed a blocking benefit. — [Carvalho & Goldstone 2014](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13421-013-0371-0.pdf)
- Study order changes which properties learners attend to and encode: sequence directs attention to differences (when successive items are from different categories) or to similarities (when from the same category). — [Carvalho & Goldstone, Frontiers 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4141442)
- Ordering instances by similarity (grouping similar instances together) improved concept learning in Weitnauer, Carvalho, Goldstone & Ritter. — [Weitnauer et al. 2013](https://pcl.sitehost.iu.edu/papers/weitnauer2013.pdf)
- Meta-analytic confirmation: the interleaving benefit grows with between-category similarity and shrinks with within-category similarity. — [Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/)
- Two learning systems (contested). Maddox, Ashby & Bohil (2003) reported that delaying feedback by a few seconds hurts "information-integration" category learning (categories that cannot be verbalised as a simple rule) but leaves rule-based learning intact. A 2005 replication that removed confounds reproduced the pattern, and found the deficit arose largely because learners under delayed feedback switched to explicit hypothesis-testing strategies. Single-system accounts have been offered as alternatives. — [Maddox & Ing 2005 record](https://scilit.com/publications/bcca8ab02cb800b962191bed3f964ade)

### Inferences
- "Which boards / hands do what" is an inductive category-learning problem. Solver output gives exemplars; the player must abstract the features (board connectedness, high-card ownership, nut advantage, blockers). The category-learning results suggest a mode that presents contrasting pairs: two near-identical spots with different solver answers, asking "what changed and why", is better supported than a long run of one texture.
- A "minimal pair" generator (hold everything fixed, change one feature: a suit, a rank, a stack depth) is the direct application of discriminative contrast, and is something a solver-backed tool can produce cheaply.
- Poker strategy has both a rule-like layer (verbalisable heuristics: "bet small with range on dry ace-high boards") and an intuition layer (weighing many cues at once). If the two-systems account holds, rule-like content tolerates delayed, explanatory feedback, while intuitive pattern recognition is trained better by fast feedback after each response. This argues for offering both: a fast "reps" mode with immediate answer display, and a slower "review" mode with explanation.
- Since learners rate blocked study as more effective even after it has demonstrably failed them, a user-facing toggle "drill one texture at a time" will be popular and should not be the default beyond an introductory phase.

### Gaps
- The perceptual-learning-module literature (Kellman and colleagues: many short classification trials with feedback to train pattern recognition in maths, aviation, medical imaging) is highly relevant to board-texture recognition; a Mettler, Massey & Kellman (2016) paper on adaptive vs fixed practice schedules surfaced in search ([PDF](https://kellmanlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/357/2025/09/mettler_massey_kellman_2016.pdf)) but its findings were not read in this pass.
- No inductive-learning study was found using categories with graded (probabilistic, mixed-frequency) membership of the kind a solver produces.
- Self-explanation and elaborative interrogation (moderate utility in Dunlosky) were not researched beyond their rating.

## 4. Learning in noisy, probabilistic environments; process vs outcome feedback

### Takeaway
When outcomes are only loosely tied to decision quality, outcome feedback teaches the wrong lessons: people (including when told otherwise) judge decisions by results, and in multiple-cue probability learning it is information about the task's true structure, not outcome feedback, that improves judgement. Poker results are a textbook "wicked" feedback signal; solver-derived expected value and frequency information is the "task information" the literature says is needed.

### Cited Findings
- Kind vs wicked learning environments (Hogarth, Lejarraga & Soyer 2015, "The two settings of kind and wicked learning environments"): inference has a learning setting and an application setting. In kind environments the information in the two matches, which is a necessary condition for accurate inference; in wicked environments there are mismatches (feedback missing, biased or misleading), and experience teaches wrong or incomplete lessons. — [summary quoting the abstract](https://channingwalton.github.io/posts/2024-10-19/kind-and-wicked)
- Outcome bias (Baron & Hershey 1988, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 54(4), 569-579): across 5 studies, people rated the quality of thinking and the competence of the decision maker higher when the outcome was favourable, although they had all the information the decision maker had; in monetary gambles the reasoning was rated better when the unchosen option turned out badly. — [Baron & Hershey 1988 PDF](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/baron-hershey-jpsp1988.pdf)
- Cognitive feedback vs outcome feedback. Balzer, Doherty & O'Connor (1989, Psychological Bulletin 106, 410-433) decomposed cognitive feedback into task information (how cues actually relate to the criterion), cognitive information (how the learner is weighting cues) and functional validity information. Their review, and a later direct test by Sulsky and colleagues, found task information is the component responsible for improved judgement in multiple-cue probability learning. — [Sulsky et al. record](https://researchwith.montclair.edu/en/publications/task-information-cognitive-information-or-functional-validity-inf/)
- Feedback delay in probabilistic/implicit category learning shifts people toward explicit rule-testing strategies and lowers accuracy for categories that require integrating several cues. — [Maddox & Ing 2005 record](https://scilit.com/publications/bcca8ab02cb800b962191bed3f964ade)
- Poker-specific evidence is thin. One study (Linnet et al. 2010) found experienced poker players had smaller estimation and decision bias than inexperienced players in probability estimation, despite overestimating more on accepted gambles. It does not test outcome bias. "Resulting" as a name for outcome bias in poker comes from a practitioner book (Duke, Thinking in Bets), not from a study. — [Linnet et al. 2010](https://cdspress.ca/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/Jakob-Linnet-Line-Gebauer-Howard-Shaffer-Kim-Mouridsen-Arne-Moller-.pdf)

### Inferences
- A trainer should grade the decision, never the runout. Showing "you won the pot" or dealing out the remaining cards after a drill answer reintroduces outcome feedback and, by the Baron & Hershey result, will colour the user's judgement of their own choice even if they know better. If a play-through mode shows results at all, the EV-based assessment should come first and be visually dominant.
- Solver output is "task information" in Balzer's sense: it tells the learner the true cue-criterion relations (which features of the spot move the strategy). The most valuable feedback is therefore not "the solver bets 62%" but which features drive that (range advantage, nut advantage, blockers). Aggregated reports across many spots ("on paired boards you under-bluff by X") are the "cognitive information" component: how the learner's own weighting differs from the correct one.
- Mixed strategies make single-trial right/wrong grading itself a noisy outcome signal. If the solver bets 60% and checks 40%, marking a check "wrong" is misleading, and marking either action "right" carries almost no information. Better-supported designs given the above: (a) ask for the frequency (or a frequency bucket) rather than an action, and score distance from the solver frequency; (b) score by EV loss, so indifferent mixes cost nothing and pure errors cost a lot; (c) score at range level (ask how a class of hands plays) rather than hand level; (d) aggregate over many answers and feed back the user's own frequencies against the solver's. These are inferences from the feedback and probability-learning literature; none has been tested in poker.
- Probability matching and frequency estimation are known human weaknesses in probabilistic tasks (background knowledge, not sourced here), which is a further reason to give explicit frequency feedback rather than expecting frequencies to be inferred from sampled actions.
- Real-money play is a wicked environment; the trainer is valuable precisely because it can be made kind: immediate, accurate, decision-level feedback on a representative sample of spots. Representativeness matters under the Hogarth framing: if the drill's spot distribution (or the solver's assumptions about the opponent) differs from what the user meets at the table, the learning setting and application setting mismatch.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed experiment was found on outcome bias in poker players, or on training poker decisions with EV feedback vs result feedback. This is a genuine gap in the literature as far as this search could tell.
- The weather-prediction-task literature (Knowlton, Gluck and colleagues) and the comparison of observational vs feedback-based training in probabilistic category learning were searched for but not retrieved.
- Hogarth's original paper was only seen through its abstract in a secondary source; details of his taxonomy (e.g. sampling vs feedback mismatches) were not verified.
- The specific claim that deliberate, EV-based review transfers to table performance has no direct evidence found here.

## 5. Spaced-repetition scheduling algorithms and whether they suit skills rather than facts

### Takeaway
The peer-reviewed literature supports spacing and criterion-based relearning far more firmly than it supports any particular algorithm: expanding schedules have not been shown to beat equal intervals, and the evidence that FSRS outperforms SM-2 is a predictive-accuracy benchmark on flashcard review logs run by the algorithm's own community, not a controlled learning-outcome trial. All these schedulers model the memory strength of a single atomic item, which fits poker facts (ranges, sizings) but only loosely fits a generalisable skill.

### Cited Findings
- Optimal spacing depends on how long the knowledge must last, and too-short gaps cost more than too-long gaps. — [Cepeda et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/)
- Expanding (growing) intervals, the core assumption of SM-2-style and Leitner-style schedulers, produced recall equivalent to equal intervals at 8 weeks in a multi-day experiment; earlier comparisons were mostly within one session and often found no expanding advantage. — [Kang et al. 2014](https://home.cs.colorado.edu/~mozer/Research/Selected%20Publications/reprints/KangLindseyMozerPashler2014.pdf)
- Successive relearning (practise to a criterion with feedback, then relearn in later spaced sessions) has classroom evidence for durable gains. — [SecEd guide summarising Rawson & Dunlosky](https://www.sec-ed.co.uk/content/best-practice/a-teacher-s-guide-to-retrieval-practice-successive-relearning)
- FSRS: grew out of a 2022 KDD paper by Ye and colleagues using stochastic dynamic programming (stochastic shortest path) to schedule reviews. (Community history, not the paper itself.) — [history of FSRS](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/G7fpGCi8r7nCKXsQk/the-history-of-fsrs-for-anki)
- The open spaced-repetition benchmark evaluates algorithms on roughly 10,000 Anki collections (hundreds of millions of reviews) by log loss of predicted recall. FSRS with per-user optimisation has lower log loss than SM-2 in about 99% of collections. The benchmark authors note SM-2 was never designed to output probabilities, so extra formulas had to be bolted on to compare it. This is self-published by the FSRS community, not peer reviewed. — [benchmark write-up](https://expertium.github.io/Benchmark.html)
- For maths skills, spacing and interleaving of problem types produced a large delayed-test gain in a classroom RCT (d = 0.83), showing spacing/mixing applies to procedural skill, not only facts. — [WWC review of Rohrer et al.](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Study/88770)

### Inferences
- What is well supported: (1) come back to material after a delay; (2) the delay should scale with how long retention is needed; (3) err on the side of longer gaps; (4) practise to a correct response with feedback and repeat across sessions. What is not established: that an optimised algorithm produces measurably better learning than a simple schedule (e.g. fixed expanding steps or Leitner boxes). Better recall prediction (FSRS) plausibly means fewer wasted reviews for the same retention, but that is an efficiency argument inferred from a prediction benchmark, not a demonstrated outcome gain.
- Unit-of-scheduling problem. Flashcard schedulers assume the item reviewed is the item to be remembered. In poker the target is a rule that generalises across thousands of spots; re-showing the identical spot risks memorising the answer to that card combination. The better fit is to schedule at the level of a concept or spot class (e.g. "c-bet on monotone boards out of position") and sample a fresh instance each time. The memory-strength estimate then attaches to the class.
- Grading problem. SM-2/FSRS take a pass/fail or 1-4 self-rating. With mixed-frequency answers and noisy correctness, a single trial is weak evidence of mastery; a class-level scheduler should update on a rolling accuracy or mean EV loss over several instances, not on one response.
- Response time is a usable extra signal for skills (fast and correct suggests automaticity; slow and correct suggests fragile knowledge). This matches the low-confidence-correct finding in section 1 and the adaptive-schedule work flagged in section 3 gaps, but is not sourced here.
- A pragmatic option set for the owner: (a) simple Leitner boxes per spot class (cheap, transparent, defensible); (b) FSRS per spot class with fresh instances (more efficient in principle, but its parameters were fitted to flashcard data and its fit to skill classes is untested); (c) error-weighted sampling without an explicit memory model (pick classes in proportion to recent EV loss, with a minimum gap). There is no evidence found that ranks these for skill learning.

### Gaps
- The Ye et al. KDD 2022 paper and any peer-reviewed evaluation of FSRS were not retrieved; no randomised comparison of scheduling algorithms on learning outcomes was found.
- No peer-reviewed evaluation of SM-2 or the Leitner system as such was found in this pass.
- No evidence found on spaced-repetition algorithms applied to procedural or strategic skills with variable instances; this appears to be an open question rather than a settled one.
- Lindsey, Shroyer, Pashler & Mozer (2014), a classroom study of personalised review scheduling, is known from background knowledge to be relevant but was not retrieved.

## 6. Concrete implications for a drill / trainer

### Takeaway
The evidence supports a trainer built around answer-first drilling on fresh instances of spaced, deliberately confusable spot classes, with EV-and-frequency-based explanatory feedback, confidence capture to target feedback and expose miscalibration, and guidance that fades as the user improves; and it warns that users will prefer the less effective variants.

### Cited Findings
(Each implication below rests on findings already cited above; the anchor source is repeated.)
- Answer before reveal: testing beats restudy by about g = 0.5, more with feedback. — [Pan & Sana 2021 citing Rowland](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PS_2021.pdf)
- Explanations beat right/wrong: 0.49 vs 0.05. — [Van der Kleij et al. 2015 record](https://scinapse.io/papers/2110661236)
- Space by required retention; too short is worse than too long. — [Cepeda et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/)
- Interleave similar categories; block dissimilar ones. — [Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/); [Carvalho & Goldstone 2014](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13421-013-0371-0.pdf)
- Practice performance misleads; users prefer massing. — [Soderstrom & Bjork 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25910388/); [Kornell & Bjork 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18578849/)
- High-confidence errors are the most correctable; low-confidence correct answers need confirmation. — [Butterfield & Metcalfe 2006](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11409-006-6894-z); [review citing Butler et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3970786)
- Calibration improves only with feedback on both performance and judgements. — [Callender et al. 2016](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11409-015-9142-6)
- Fade guidance adaptively. — [Salden et al. 2010](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11251-009-9107-8)
- Task information, not outcome feedback, improves probabilistic judgement; outcomes bias evaluation. — [Sulsky et al. record](https://researchwith.montclair.edu/en/publications/task-information-cognitive-information-or-functional-validity-inf/); [Baron & Hershey 1988](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/baron-hershey-jpsp1988.pdf)

### Inferences
Question selection
- Select by spot class, generate a new instance each time; never rely on the identical hand/board recurring.
- Weight selection toward classes with high recent EV loss and toward classes that are due by spacing; keep a floor of mastered classes in the mix so they are not dropped.
- Deliberately pair confusable classes (minimal pairs differing in one feature) rather than sampling uniformly.
- Sample spots in proportion to how often they arise and how much EV is at stake, so the practice distribution matches the game (kind-environment argument).

Spacing
- Within a session, do not repeat a class back to back; return to it after other classes intervene.
- Across sessions, re-present a class at growing or fixed gaps of days; there is no good evidence that the exact schedule shape matters much, so choose the simplest that is transparent. Longer is safer than shorter.
- Use a mastery criterion per class per session (e.g. a run of low-EV-loss answers), then bring it back in a later session (successive relearning).

Mixing spot types (candidate study modes)
- Learn mode (novice or new concept): short explanation or worked example, then a brief blocked run on one class. Appropriate early, harmful as the main mode.
- Contrast mode: interleaved confusable classes or side-by-side minimal pairs, "what changed?" prompts. Best supported for learning which boards/hands do what.
- Mixed review mode: spaced, interleaved drilling across all learned classes; the long-run default.
- Fast reps mode: many quick classification trials with immediate answer display, aimed at pattern recognition.
- Deep review mode: fewer spots, user states reasoning or predicts range-level frequencies before seeing the solver explanation.

When to show explanations
- Always after the user commits, never before (in drill modes).
- Always available; show by default after errors, after low-confidence correct answers, and for new classes. Keep them short and about the features that drive the strategy (task information).
- Feedback timing: the evidence is mixed. Immediate feedback per answer is safe and suits fast pattern learning; an end-of-session summary or next-day revisit of the session's errors adds a spaced second exposure and has some support for transfer. Offering both is consistent with the evidence; nothing found supports withholding feedback altogether.
- For mixed spots, show the full frequency split and the EV of each action; do not call a solver-mixed action wrong.

Asking for confidence
- A one-tap confidence rating (or asking for a frequency estimate, which doubles as a confidence statement) before reveal lets the tool (a) highlight high-confidence errors, where correction is most effective, (b) reinforce low-confidence correct answers, and (c) show the user a calibration curve over time. Calibration only improves when the user sees judgement and performance together.
- Cost: an extra tap per question slows the rep rate; make it optional or periodic in fast modes.

Metrics and messaging
- Report delayed accuracy / EV loss by class as the headline progress measure, not session score. Tell users that mixed, spaced drilling feels harder and scores lower in-session; that is expected.
- Keep feedback on the decision and the reasoning; avoid framing that centres the player's standing or ego.

### Gaps
- None of the above has been tested in poker or any comparable imperfect-information game; the transfer from school maths, perceptual categories and verbal learning is an inference.
- Optimal session length, number of classes per session, and criterion level have no specific evidence here.
- Whether asking for frequencies (vs a single action) improves learning of mixed strategies is untested.
- Motivation and adherence (streaks, gamification) were out of scope; note only that the most effective modes are the ones users rate lowest, which is an adherence risk the report writer may want to flag.
