## A1. Does the representation or tool people learn from determine what experts can do, and does a redesigned representation change performance?

### Takeaway
Yes, with good evidence in several fields: the same information in a different format changes success rates several-fold (Bayesian problems: roughly 4% correct in probability format vs 24% in natural frequencies), experts' capacity limits follow the structure of their tool (mental-abacus users are limited by columns, not quantity), and the arrival of a new tool measurably changed elite play in Go. The chess evidence is weaker and contested: elite move quality rose steadily over four decades without a clear jump at engine milestones.

### Cited Findings

**Notation as part of the computation**
- Zhang & Norman (1995, Cognition 57:271-295) analyse numeral systems as "distributed representations" split between the mind and the page; using multiplication as the worked case, they argue Arabic numerals are effective because more of the needed information is carried externally by the notation, where sign-value systems such as Roman numerals leave it to be held internally. Theoretical/representational analysis, not a training experiment (abstract and citing works only). — [Zhang & Norman 1995, DOI record](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0277(95)00674-3); [1993 conference precursor](https://escholarship.org/content/qt7wr256pq/qt7wr256pq.pdf)
- Hutchins (1995, Cognitive Science 19:265-288): in an airliner cockpit, speed bugs on the airspeed dial turn a memory-and-comparison task into a perceptual one (is the needle between the bugs?). His claim: "Speed bugs do not help pilots remember speeds; rather, they are part of the process by which the cockpit system remembers speeds", and the cognitive properties of the system "differ radically" from those of the individuals in it. Ethnographic/analytic, not experimental; pinpoint quote seen via secondary summaries. — [Hutchins 1995 PDF](https://courses.ischool.berkeley.edu/i290-3/s05/papers/How_a_cockpit_remembers_its_speeds.pdf); [reading guide](https://hci.ucsd.edu/102b/ReadingGuidance/Hutchins.htm)

**Expert cognition takes the shape of the tool**
- Frank & Barner (2012, JEP: General 141:134-149): children trained in mental abacus do exact arithmetic on a visual image of the device. Their performance drops sharply when problems need more columns, while more beads per column costs nothing, so the limit of the skill is set by the device's column structure and ordinary visual working memory. Verbal interference barely disturbs abacus users but nearly stops untrained people calculating; motor interference had a similar slight effect (motor link tentative). — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21767040/); [press summary](https://phys.org/news/2011-08-stanford-explores-language.html)

**New tools changing elite play**
- Go: Shin, Kim, van Opheusden & Griffiths (2023, PNAS 120(12) e2214840120) scored over 5.8 million professional moves from 1950 to 2021 with a superhuman engine. Decision quality improved little year to year before 2016 and rose significantly after superhuman AI arrived; novel (previously unobserved) moves became more frequent and became associated with higher quality. The causal story (AI pushed players off traditional lines into exploration) is the authors' interpretation of a before/after pattern. — [arXiv version](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.07462v2); [Princeton record](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/superhuman-artificial-intelligence-can-improve-human-decision-mak/)
- Go, second study (press release only): players' decision quality rose when they used AI-suggested strategies, and younger players adapted faster and gained more than older ones. — [University at Albany release](https://albany.edu/business/news/2025-ai-shapes-human-decision-making-go)
- Chess (contested): Bilalić, Graf & Vaci (2024, British Journal of Psychology) analysed over 11.6 million elite decisions. One summary reports steady improvement in move quality over four decades with the two AI milestones (PC engines in the late 1990s, neural-network engines in the late 2010s) not clearly visible, and that neural engines have "not significantly impacted the best players, at least not yet"; another summary of the same paper says AI "profoundly changed" elite behaviour with improvements at both eras. I could not open the paper to resolve this. — [blog summary of the paper](https://www.chess.com/blog/bjbalas/science-of-chess-the-ai-revolution-hasnt-made-chess-better-yet); [repository record](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/220321); [author's summary](https://nemanjavaci.netlify.app/posts/aidecisions)
- Chess homogenisation (opinion, not measurement): a survey-based paper reports community concern that engine-driven preparation is "fostering a homogenization of play". — [arXiv 2602.04447](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.04447)

**Redesigned formats changing performance**
- Bayesian reasoning: the McDowell & Jacobs (2017, Psychological Bulletin 143:1273-1312) meta-analysis is reported as finding about 24% correct with natural frequencies vs about 4% with conditional probabilities, and that "a non-trivial amount of people" still fail in the better format. Figures seen via a citing paper, not the meta-analysis itself. — [Weber et al. 2018, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6194348/); [authors' conference abstract](https://smdm.confex.com/smdm/16BEC/webprogram/Paper9915.html)
- The original Gigerenzer & Hoffrage (1995) effect was larger (about 16% vs 46%); this comparison comes from a blog, so treat the pair of numbers as secondary. — [blog](https://wildetruth.substack.com/p/lowered-expectations-rarity-roulette)
- Teaching the representation beats teaching the rule: Sedlmeier & Gigerenzer (2001, JEP: General 130:380-400) trained people by computer either to construct frequency representations or to insert probabilities into Bayes's rule; representation training did better. — [abstract record](https://causeweb.org/cause/node/6925)
- Law-encoding diagrams: Cheng's AVOW diagrams for electricity (2002, Cognitive Science) outperformed the conventional equations approach on recall, multiple-choice and transfer problems with novices given matched mini-curricula; his Probability Space diagrams (2011, Topics in Cognitive Science) were reported superior to the conventional approach for learning probability. All from the inventor's own lab; I found no independent replication. — [Cheng 2002](https://users.sussex.ac.uk/~peterch/papers/Cheng-CogSci26%286%29.pdf); [Cheng 2011](https://users.sussex.ac.uk/~peterch/papers/Cheng-TopiCS-2011.pdf)
- Risk communication: Garcia-Retamero & Cokely's systematic review (2017, Human Factors 59:582-627) concludes icon arrays are the best "all purpose" visual aid on 20 years of data; "less is more" (very simple arrays with clear explanations); static aids help low-numeracy people only if they have moderate-to-high graph literacy; brief training in reading icon arrays extends the benefit. Seen via press releases. — [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/897052); [APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/obsonline/visual-aids-can-help-people-better-understand-health-risks.html)
- Making viewers work on the display helps: Okan, Garcia-Retamero, Cokely & Maldonado (2015) found explanatory labels helped less graph-literate people, while reflective questions about the icon array gave "large and robust" benefits at both low and high graph literacy. — [record](https://digitalcommons.mtu.edu/michigantech-p/8393)
- Weather: across 120 trials, people shown uncertainty estimates with a low-temperature forecast took precautions more often when appropriate and withheld them more often when appropriate than people shown a deterministic forecast; adding a decision recommendation gave no extra gain, and a recommendation alone was no better than the deterministic forecast (Joslyn & LeClerc; published as JEP: Applied 18:126-140, 2012). — [AMS conference abstract](https://ams.confex.com/ams/91Annual/webprogram/Paper182589.html); [paper record](https://www.sci.utah.edu/~kpotter/Library/Papers/joslyn:2012:UFID/index.html)

### Inferences
- The 13x13 grid is a notation in Zhang and Norman's sense: it makes some operations perceptual (which hand classes are in a range, how suited/offsuit/pair classes are arranged) and leaves others to be computed in the head. The quantity the learner most needs, "what fraction of this range bets", is not directly readable from it, because cells stand for different numbers of combinations (6 pairs, 4 suited, 12 offsuit) and mixed cells need mental weighting. On the Hutchins reading, a learner using the grid has learned a grid-plus-person skill, and what the person alone carries may be small.
- The mental-abacus result suggests that what the study tool chunks by, the expert will think by. Players trained on the grid will likely think in hand-class cells and grid regions; if the decision at the table is driven by something else (board texture, blockers, the overall frequency), the trained chunks may be the wrong unit. This is a hypothesis; no study of poker players' representations was found.
- The natural-frequency and icon-array literature points to expressing range frequencies as counts of concrete cases ("40 of these 100 combos bet") rather than percentages or colour-fill fractions, and to using a countable unit display rather than a continuous curve. The meta-analytic caveat applies: the format lifted success to about a quarter of people, not to most, so format alone will not be enough without practice.
- Sedlmeier and Gigerenzer plus Okan et al. suggest the learner should construct or interrogate the representation (build the range, answer a question about it) rather than only read it.
- The Joslyn result argues for showing the mixing frequency itself rather than a single recommended action: an explicit probability improved decisions, a bare recommendation did not.
- The Go evidence shows a solver-like tool can raise human decision quality and widen the set of moves considered, so the tool-shapes-practice effect is not inherently bad; the chess evidence warns that the size and timing of such effects is hard to establish.

### Gaps
- "Law of the instrument" (Kaplan, Maslow): I found no empirical study quantifying tool-driven practice under that name; it remains an aphorism in these notes.
- No study found on opening preparation specifically, or on players imitating engine evaluations, with measured outcomes. Recalled, unverified: work on the Maia chess models and on "centipawn loss" trends exists, but I did not confirm outcome data.
- Kirsh's work on distributed cognition (e.g. epistemic actions with Maglio, "the intelligent use of space") was not searched this session.
- Gigerenzer & Hoffrage 1995 and McDowell & Jacobs 2017 were not read directly. Recalled, unverified: Sedlmeier & Gigerenzer reported that frequency-representation training held up at follow-up some weeks later while rule training decayed.
- No research was found on the 13x13 grid or equity curves as learning representations; the poker application is untested.
- Bilalić et al. (2024) needs a direct read to settle the conflicting summaries; eprints.whiterose.ac.uk would have to be allowed in the session's network settings.

## A2. Do people become dependent on a display so that skill does not transfer when it is removed, and which designs avoid it?

### Takeaway
There is suggestive but not conclusive evidence of dependence: heavier GPS use tracks worse unaided spatial memory, and automation complacency appears in experts as well as novices and is not removed by practice or instructions. The designs with experimental support change what the aid makes the user process (landmark or reference-based guidance instead of turn-by-turn) rather than merely warning the user.

### Cited Findings
- Dahmani & Bohbot (2020, Scientific Reports): in 50 regular drivers, greater lifetime GPS use was associated with worse spatial memory during self-guided navigation; in 13 people retested after three years, greater GPS use since first testing was associated with steeper decline in hippocampus-dependent spatial memory. Heavier users did not report a poorer sense of direction, which the authors take as evidence against reverse causation. Correlational; the longitudinal arm is 13 people and was not part of the original design. — [paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7156656/); [2024 review noting the limitation](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41235-024-00545-x)
- Experimental evidence on aid design: turn-by-turn aids performed worse than reference-based guidance for building spatial awareness in a within-subjects VR study (26 participants), which the authors attribute to reliance on automated instructions. — [Kuo et al. 2022, IJHCI](https://people.cs.nycu.edu.tw/~armuro/pubs/kuo-et-al-2022-ijhci.pdf)
- A redesigned aid that keeps a distant global landmark in view led participants to process more spatial information than the standard beacon/turn-by-turn design. — [npj Science of Learning 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9296625/)
- In three driving experiments, standard navigation instructions suppressed spatial learning and landmark-based instructions overcame this. — [Overcoming Spatial Deskilling, UTS record](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/136597)
- Parasuraman & Manzey (2010, Human Factors 52:381-410), review: automation complacency arises under multiple-task load, "is found in both naive and expert participants and cannot be overcome with simple practice"; automation bias produces omission and commission errors when aids are imperfect and "cannot be prevented by training or instructions"; attention is the common mechanism. — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21077562/)
- Transfer failure without any display: world-class poker players, bridge professionals and professional soccer players did not play closer to minimax than students in unfamiliar lab games; the authors' leading explanation is that professionals cannot carry randomisation skill from the familiar field context into a new one. — [Levitt, List & Reiley 2010, NBER w15609](https://nber.org/papers/w15609)

### Inferences
- A study tool that always shows the answer display (the solved grid) is the analogue of turn-by-turn navigation: it can be followed without building the internal map. The navigation experiments suggest the remedy is a display that makes the learner process the structure (why this region bets, what anchors the frequency) rather than a display with a warning attached, since instructions and practice did not remove complacency.
- Poker differs from navigation and cockpits in one decisive way: the display is never available at the table. So study should end in conditions with the display removed, and success should be measured there. A study session that only measures performance with the chart visible measures the person-plus-chart system.
- Levitt et al. is a direct caution about transfer in poker players themselves: skill learned in one context did not show up in another. Training that varies the surface (formats, bet sizes, positions, presentation) is more likely to yield a skill that survives the move from trainer to table. This follows from the general transfer literature rather than from a poker experiment.
- The GPS evidence is too weak to claim that chart use harms unaided skill; the defensible claim is narrower: there is no evidence that reading a display builds the unaided skill, and some that it does not.

### Gaps
- Calculators: the Ellington (2003) meta-analysis was not retrieved. Recalled, unverified: it found no harm to computational skill when calculators were integrated into teaching and testing.
- Fading, generation and "desirable difficulty" (Bjork; the generation effect of Slamecka & Graf; retrieval practice of Roediger & Karpicke; the "assistance dilemma" of Koedinger & Aleven; faded worked examples of Renkl & Atkinson) were named in the brief but my search returned nothing on them, so no sourced finding is given. Recalled, unverified: these lines broadly show that generating or retrieving an answer before seeing it improves retention and transfer over re-reading, and that support should be withdrawn gradually; the report writer should source them from another researcher's notes or treat them as unsourced here.
- No experiment was found that removes a decision display after training and measures retained skill in a probabilistic judgement task.

## B1. How well can calibration be trained, how fast, and does it transfer?

### Takeaway
Calibration improves quickly with outcome feedback (most of the gain after the first round of feedback in the classic study) and is very good in professionals who forecast repeatedly with prompt, unambiguous outcomes, as weather forecasters do. Short training in forecasting tournaments gave modest gains (6 to 11% better Brier scores from under an hour of training). Transfer beyond similar tasks is limited.

### Cited Findings
- Weather forecasters: Murphy & Winkler (1977, JRSS Series C 26:41-47) report on US operational and experimental programmes of subjective probability forecasts for precipitation and temperature and conclude that forecasters "can formulate such forecasts in a reliable manner" (reliable = calibrated). A companion experiment in an area with strong local effects found forecasts "quite reliable and accurate" with good discrimination between points. Abstracts only. — [Murphy & Winkler 1977](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jorssc:v:26:y:1977:i:1:p:41-47); [Rapid City experiment record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1131758)
- Lichtenstein & Fischhoff (1980, Organizational Behavior and Human Performance 26:149-171): the first experiment gave 11 sessions of 200 probability assessments, each followed by comprehensive feedback; there was "considerable learning almost all of which was accomplished after receipt of the first feedback". The second experiment showed the same pattern and "limited generalization". The authors left open why it worked, why it did not always generalise, and why some people needed no training. — [technical report PDF](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA089619.pdf); [record](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/lichtenstein-fischhoff-trainingforcalibration-1980)
- Good Judgment Project: Mellers et al. (2014, Psychological Science 25:1106-1115) name three drivers of accuracy, training, teaming and tracking, and report that they improved both calibration and resolution. Training covered reducing cognitive biases, using reference classes, and heuristics such as averaging several estimates. The tournament's official metric was the cumulative Brier score. — [Mellers et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614524255); [Tetlock et al. 2014 on the tournament](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/07/2014---forecasting-tournaments-tools-for-increasing-transparency-and-improving-debate.pdf)
- Chang, Chen, Mellers & Tetlock (2016, Judgment and Decision Making 11:509-526): a debiasing module (CHAMPS KNOW) lasting under one hour "consistently improved accuracy (Brier scores) by 6 to 11% over the control condition" across a four-year tournament; cognitive ability and practice contributed largely independently; the authors call the effects likely lower bounds because the tutorial was brief, and they could not say which components of the module did the work. — [abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:cup:judgdm:v:11:y:2016:i:5:p:509-526_8); [full text](https://cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/123EB18425391D05FA6581FDBB3F309F/S1930297500004599a.pdf/developing-expert-political-judgment-the-impact-of-training-and-practice-on-judgmental-accuracy-in-geopolitical-forecasting-tournaments.pdf)
- Transfer: domain-specific performance feedback is described as the most effective debiasing technique; a 2019 study found feedback training effective within general knowledge with "only limited transfer" to a specific technical domain (petroleum engineering). — [CogSci 2019 paper](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4t13h8bn); [related thesis record](https://web4.library.adelaide.edu.au/dspace/handle/2440/137185)
- Transfer, further example: feedback about overconfidence on general-knowledge questions lowered confidence on an eyewitness-memory task without improving calibration or resolution there. — [Bornstein & Zickafoose](https://home.csulb.edu/~cwallis/382/certainty/overconfidence/I%20know%20I%20know.pdf)

### Inferences
- A poker trainer can reproduce the weather forecaster's conditions better than almost any real job: the true frequency is known from the solver, so every estimate ("this range bets what percent here?") can be scored immediately and without noise. Real play gives none of this (the opponent's range is rarely shown, outcomes are noisy), which may explain why table experience alone leaves frequency estimates poor. This is the strongest design implication in this section.
- Because most of the calibration gain came after the first feedback, a short calibration module is likely worth building early; it should show the learner their own calibration curve (stated frequency against true frequency), since comprehensive feedback of that kind is what the classic study used.
- Because transfer is limited, calibration should be trained on poker frequency questions directly, and across the spot types that matter (different streets, positions, textures), rather than on general trivia, and it should not be assumed to carry from one family of spots to another.
- The GJP effect size (6 to 11%) sets expectations for instruction alone: modest. Practice with scored feedback contributed separately, so the trainer should rely on volume of scored estimates more than on a tutorial.
- Calibration and resolution are distinct. A learner who answers "about 50%" everywhere may be calibrated on average yet useless; feedback should reward telling high-frequency spots from low-frequency ones.

### Gaps
- Calibration training in medicine and in intelligence analysis: no primary study was retrieved. Recalled, unverified: Christensen-Szalanski & Bushyhead (1981) found physicians overestimated pneumonia probability; Mandel & Barnes (2014, PNAS) found Canadian intelligence forecasts well calibrated and slightly underconfident.
- Mellers et al. 2014 exact effect sizes were not retrieved (full text not opened). Recalled, unverified: year-one conditions compared probabilistic-reasoning training with scenario training, with the former doing better, and teams beating individuals.
- Whether the 6 to 11% is a relative Brier improvement was not verified in the tables.
- How long calibration gains last without continued feedback was not found.
- Recalled, unverified: Keren (1987) found expert bridge players well calibrated about making contracts, the nearest card-game analogue; no calibration study of poker players was found.

## B2. Can people learn frequencies, stop probability matching, and produce mixed strategies?

### Takeaway
People pick up event frequencies with little effort, but what they do with them is poor by default: they probability-match instead of maximising, underweight rare events learned from small samples, and generate "random" sequences that switch too often. Each of these improves with the right intervention (a hint that maximising exists, long training with feedback and incentives, statistical feedback on one's own sequences), yet even professionals who mix well in their own game show serial dependence and fail to transfer the skill to unfamiliar games.

### Cited Findings

**Encoding frequency**
- Hasher & Zacks (1984, American Psychologist 39:1372-1388) review evidence that frequency of occurrence is stored by an implicit, automatic encoding process for a wide range of naturally occurring events; their earlier work claimed it is unaffected by intention, practice, depression or age. — [abstract](https://hasherlab.psych.utoronto.ca/abstracts/zacks_amerpsych_84.htm); [1979 study](https://hasherlab.psych.utoronto.ca/abstracts/Zacks_79.htm)
- Contested: Fisk (1986) argued frequency encoding is neither inevitable nor automatic, and later work found impaired frequency judgements in head-injured and elderly groups. — [review of the dispute](https://uva.theopenscholar.com/files/cogage/files/frequency-of-occurrence_8.pdf)

**Experience vs description**
- Wulff, Mergenthaler-Canseco & Hertwig (2018, Psychological Bulletin 144:140-176), meta-analysis of over 70,000 choices by over 6,000 participants: the description-experience gap is robust; people behave as if they overweight rare events when told the probabilities and underweight them when they learn by sampling; reliance on small samples is the largest determinant, because rare events are under-represented in few draws. Effect size varies widely across studies. — [Wulff et al. 2018](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_2548464_4/component/file_2552718/id10007296.pdf); [later replication noting heterogeneity](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11166-022-09393-w)

**Matching vs maximising**
- Shanks, Tunney & McCarthy (2002, Journal of Behavioral Decision Making 15:233-250): in three experiments combining large financial incentives, regular feedback and extensive training, large proportions of participants came to maximise, and each of the three factors contributed. Abstract only; proportions not retrieved. — [record](https://research.aston.ac.uk/en/publications/a-re-examination-of-probability-matching-and-rational-choice/)
- Koehler & James (2010, Memory & Cognition 38:667-676): matching arises because the matching strategy comes to mind easily and maximising does not; when maximising was brought to participants' attention, more of them adopted it. They describe many matchers as "underthinking". — [paper](https://dx.doi.org/10.3758/MC.38.6.667)
- Newell et al. (2013): a hint making maximising available raises it early in the task; feedback works on a slower timescale. — [Newell et al., Memory & Cognition](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758%2Fs13421-012-0268-3.pdf)

**Producing randomness and mixed strategies**
- People asked to generate or recognise random sequences typically produce negative autocorrelation (too many alternations). — [Gauriot, Page & Wooders, Nash at Wimbledon](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/116715/1/SSRN-id2850919.pdf)
- Neuringer (1986, JEP: General 115:62-75): students typing sequences of two digits in trials of 100 responses, given feedback on 5 or 10 statistical descriptors after each trial, learned to produce sequences that those statistics could not tell from computer-generated random numbers. Earlier studies that only instructed people to be random had failed. Critics argue the effect may be mediated by instructions and is not simple reinforcement. — [Neuringer 1986 PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/1986-neuringer.pdf); [critique](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3501429)
- Budescu & Rapoport (1994) compared sequence generation in one-person tasks and two-person zero-sum games: both showed similar deviations from randomness, though the games appeared to induce qualitatively different cognitive processes. — [1994 follow-up abstract](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/bdm.3960070404)
- Tennis: Walker & Wooders (2001) found win rates for top players' serve directions consistent with minimax (equal payoff across directions), but serial independence failed: servers switched direction too often. — [Walker & Wooders, Minimax Play at Wimbledon](https://www.math.stonybrook.edu/~gaston/print/Old/WimbledonAER.pdf)
- Replication at scale: with about 500,000 Hawk-Eye-tracked serves, Gauriot, Page & Wooders found win rates conform "remarkably closely" to theory for men and somewhat less for women, and serve directions are not serially independent for either; they also argue the original test was underpowered. — [Nash at Wimbledon](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/116715/1/SSRN-id2850919.pdf)
- Penalty kicks: Palacios-Huerta (2003) analysed 1,417 kicks and reported both equal success rates across strategies and serially independent choices. Seen via a secondary summary. — [summary](https://blogs.cornell.edu/info2040/2021/09/16/penalties-serves-and-being-random-in-sports)
- Lab transfer (contested): Palacios-Huerta & Volij (2007) reported soccer professionals playing near-exact minimax in lab games. Levitt, List & Reiley (2010, Econometrica 78:1413-1434) did not reproduce this: students, bridge professionals, world-class poker players and professional soccer players all made choices "generally not close to minimax", and poker professionals played "no closer to minimax than students". — [Levitt, List & Reiley, NBER w15609](https://nber.org/papers/w15609); [Econometrica record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ecm:emetrp:v:78:y:2010:i:4:p:1413-1434)

### Inferences
- Frequency intake is cheap, so a learner shown many concrete instances (this hand bets, this one checks, drawn at the true rates) should absorb "about 40%" without being told the number. The description-experience evidence adds a condition: the samples must be large enough, or low-frequency actions (a 5 to 10% raise, a rare bluff) will be learned as "never". A trainer should deliberately over-expose or explicitly state rare branches. Both points are inferences; no study tested them with poker material.
- Describing and experiencing should be combined: a stated frequency alone is likely to be mis-weighted, and experience alone under-represents rare events.
- Two different skills hide under "learn the 40% frequency". Estimating that a range bets 40% is a judgement task, where calibration training applies. Acting on a mix is a production task, where the default failure is the opposite of the matching literature's: in a mixed-strategy spot matching the solver frequency is correct, and the learner's error is to purify (always take the action that feels best) or to alternate. So the matching literature should be applied carefully: against an opponent whose tendency is fixed and known ("he folds half the time"), the right response is usually a pure best response, i.e. maximising, and learners will tend to under-exploit by matching. Koehler and James suggest that simply naming the maximising option moves many people.
- The trainer should therefore label which kind of spot the learner is in: indifferent (mix; any frequency costs little against an equilibrium opponent) or exploitable (maximise). This distinction is my inference from game theory plus the matching literature, not a tested teaching method.
- Neuringer shows randomisation can be trained with statistical feedback on one's own sequence: a trainer can show the learner their realised frequency over the last N instances of a spot and their run structure (too many alternations). The tennis and penalty data imply the realistic target: get aggregate frequencies right; serial independence is the harder part and even top professionals miss it.
- An external randomiser (a clock second hand, a card-suit rule, a random number) sidesteps the human inability to randomise. No study on this was found, so it is a design suggestion only; note that it shifts the skill from "produce randomness" to "know the frequency", which is the trainable part.
- Levitt et al. is the only evidence here involving poker professionals, and it says their randomisation did not transfer to a new game. Treat mixing as context-bound and train it in the contexts where it must be used.

### Gaps
- Rapoport & Budescu (1992, JEP: General) and their 1997 Psychological Review model were not retrieved; only the 1994 follow-up abstract was. Recalled, unverified: the 1992 paper found sequences produced while playing a competitive game were closer to random than sequences produced on instruction.
- Shanks et al.'s proportions maximising and trial counts were not retrieved. Recalled, unverified: on the order of 1,500 or more trials.
- Hertwig, Barron, Weber & Erev (2004) was seen only through citing papers.
- No study was found that trains people to play a specified mixed frequency (say 40/60) and measures accuracy of the produced frequency; Neuringer's work targets 50/50 binary randomness.
- Whether automatic frequency encoding holds for conditional frequencies (frequency of an action given a context), which is what poker needs, was not addressed by anything retrieved.

## B3. How should estimates be elicited and scored: proper scoring rules, intervals vs points, a number vs a band?

### Takeaway
Scoring probability estimates with a proper scoring rule (the Brier score) is the standard feedback in the best-performing forecasting programmes, and precise numeric answers carry real information: rounding skilled forecasters' numbers into coarse verbal bands consistently lowered accuracy. For uncertainty about a quantity, asking people to produce a confidence interval gives intervals that are too narrow; asking them to spread probability over fixed bands reduces that overprecision.

### Cited Findings
- The IARPA tournament scored forecasters by cumulative Brier score (0 is perfect, lower is better), and the training, teaming and tracking interventions that improved accuracy were all evaluated on it. — [Tetlock et al. 2014](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/07/2014---forecasting-tournaments-tools-for-increasing-transparency-and-improving-debate.pdf)
- Friedman, Baker, Mellers, Tetlock & Zeckhauser (2018, International Studies Quarterly 62:410-422), 888,328 forecasts: coarsening numeric probabilities into bins matching common qualitative expressions, including those recommended to intelligence analysts, "consistently sacrifices predictive accuracy". The result did not depend on extreme probabilities, time horizon, question type or scoring rule. Returns to precision correlated mainly with skill, effort, experience and training, not numeracy, education or cognitive style. — [HKS record](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/value-precision-probability-assessment-evidence-large-scale-geopolitical-forecasting); [working paper](https://scholar.harvard.edu/sites/scholar.harvard.edu/files/rzeckhauser/files/value_of_precision.pdf)
- Soll & Klayman (2004), as reported by Haran et al.: people's produced confidence intervals are too narrow, and judging the probability of fixed intervals yields less overconfidence than producing an interval. — [Haran, Moore & Morewedge 2010](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/34DDFA0D8B602B6089A1B6BEEF22A486/S1930297500001637a.pdf/a-simple-remedy-for-overprecision-in-judgment.pdf)
- Haran, Moore & Morewedge (2010, Judgment and Decision Making 5:467-476): in three experiments, the SPIES method (assign a probability to every interval across the whole range, summing to 100%) significantly reduced overprecision relative to asking for a confidence interval. — [paper](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/34DDFA0D8B602B6089A1B6BEEF22A486/S1930297500001637a.pdf/a-simple-remedy-for-overprecision-in-judgment.pdf)
- Lichtenstein & Fischhoff's training gave comprehensive feedback after each block of 200 assessments, i.e. aggregate calibration feedback rather than only item-by-item right/wrong. — [technical report](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA089619.pdf)

### Inferences
- Ask for a number and score it with a proper rule. For "what fraction of this range bets?" the true answer is a known frequency, so squared error against the solver frequency is the natural score (it is the Brier logic applied to a frequency), and it rewards honesty about uncertainty in a way a pass/fail tolerance does not.
- On number versus band, the evidence points both ways and the trainer can use both. For skilled estimators, bands throw away accuracy (Friedman et al.), so the answer input should allow fine granularity, with the returns to precision growing as skill grows. For expressing uncertainty, fixed bands with probabilities (SPIES) beat a self-produced interval. A workable design: a numeric answer for the estimate, and, where confidence matters, a spread of weight over bands rather than "give me a range". The Friedman result concerns geopolitical event probabilities, so its transfer to estimating a range frequency is an inference.
- A beginner's coarse bands ("rarely, sometimes, about half, mostly, always") are probably a sensible starting scaffold that should be refined as skill grows; the forecasting evidence says precision pays for the skilled, and says nothing on whether novices learn faster from bands. This is a guess and is flagged as such.
- Show aggregate feedback (a calibration plot and a running score over the last block) in addition to per-item error, since the training studies that worked used block-level feedback.
- Score resolution as well as calibration, so that hedging towards 50% is not rewarded.

### Gaps
- No direct experiment was found comparing "type a number" with "choose a band" as a learning method (as opposed to an accuracy-of-report method).
- Primary sources on proper scoring rules as feedback (Brier 1950; Gneiting & Raftery 2007) and on whether scoring-rule feedback beats plain outcome feedback for learning were not retrieved.
- Soll & Klayman (2004) was seen only through Haran et al.; its numbers were not retrieved. Recalled, unverified: 80% intervals contained the truth well under 80% of the time, and asking separately for the low and high bounds helped.
- Friedman et al.'s specific bin widths and the size of the accuracy loss were not retrieved.
